# Exhibit 016 research brief: spinach iron decimal point

Researcher pass (exhibit-researcher), then the main session read Hamblin 1981 and Sutton 2010 directly (pdftotext) and re-read Rekdal 2014 (PMC HTML). Everything under VERIFIED was read in the primary text by the main session. Publish date if built today: 2026-10-23.

## The shape

A story told for decades as the classic example of an uncorrected scientific error (a decimal point that made spinach 10 times richer in iron) has no traceable source. Several of the authors who used it were writing specifically about sloppy citation. Rekdal (2014): "By using the non-existent, or at least undocumented, decimal point error as an illustration of the importance of accuracy and critical investigation, Bender, Hamblin, Larsson, and Skrabanek and McCormick all end up doing the opposite of what they are preaching."

Country: "United Kingdom" (BMJ, Bender in London, Sutton at Nottingham Trent; matches how 013 picked the country of the publishing institutions). ## VERIFIED

### Hamblin, T.J. "Fake!" BMJ 1981;283(6307):1671-1674 (Christmas issue, 19 Dec 1981)
Read in full from https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/283/6307/1671.full.pdf (PMC record: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1507475/).
- Exact passage: "The discovery that spinach was as valuable a source of iron as red meat was made in the 1890s ... Unfortunately, the propaganda was fraudulent; German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value. Spinach is no better for you than cabbage, Brussels sprouts, or broccoli. For a source of iron Popeye would have been better off chewing the cans (fig 1)."
- Also in the passage: a Popeye statue in Crystal City, Texas; claim he raised US spinach consumption by 33% (garbled in the PDF as "33 7,"; do not use the figure).
- The paragraph has no citation. The article ends with a list of 13 publications it "derived from", none about spinach. Sutton independently counts the same 13: "Hamblin's (1981) article does in fact contain 13 references to sources, none of which, however, cite the source of the decimal error or 1930's Germans".
- The article is about scientific fraud and fakes; its framing line: "Frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes".
- Do NOT say Hamblin invented it: Bender 1972 precedes him (Rekdal).

### Sutton, M. "Spinach, Iron and Popeye: Ironic lessons from biochemistry and history on the importance of healthy eating, healthy scepticism and adequate citation." Internet Journal of Criminology, 2010.
Read in full via mirror PDF https://www.erwinmayer.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/10/Sutton_Spinach_Iron_and_Popeye_March_2010.pdf (journal site 403s). Author: Reader in Criminology, Nottingham Trent University. Note the actual subtitle ends "adequate citation" (researcher had "sloppy science").
- Sutton emailed Hamblin. Sutton reports: Hamblin "wrote that he has been asked for this before and that he honestly cannot remember. He said that back in 1981 he had been asked to write a humorous piece by the then editor of the British Medical Journal (BMJ) for its Christmas edition and that he had been asked not to provide references." And: "Hamblin rounded off his email with the assurance that he was sure that he had not made it up." (Reported via Sutton; attribute as such.)
- Sutton's 4 questions included: "Who is von Wolff and where are his erroneous findings recorded?" and "Who were the 1930s chemists who discovered the decimal place error and where...". He could not answer them after weeks of searching.
- Fresh vs dried: Sutton quotes Coultate 2009 (Royal Society of Chemistry book) giving 2 competing versions: von Bunge 1890 found 35mg per 100g but had analysed dried spinach; or von Wolf ~20 years earlier misplaced the decimal point. "Wherever lies the truth, the error was not picked up until 1937". Sutton cites dried-spinach measurements of 44.8 mg/100g (Jackson 1938) and about 34 mg/100g (Oladele and Obarisaide 2009). This is the measurement-definitions angle: the high figure may be a correct measurement of dried spinach, read as fresh.
- Popeye: Sutton read the bound volumes of the Thimble Theatre strips 1928 to 1935 and reports that the strip of 3 July 1932, the first where Popeye explains why he eats spinach, credits vitamin A, and that of "hundreds of Popeye cartoons studied" none associates spinach with iron. Abstract: "In fact, Segar chose and promoted spinach for its vitamin A content alone." (Sutton's finding; report as his.)

### Rekdal, O.B. "Academic urban legends." Social Studies of Science 2014;44(4):638-654. doi:10.1177/0306312714535679. Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4232290/
- Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture, Queen Elizabeth College, University of London: "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point" (Bender, 1972: 11). Earliest found version, and hedged.
- Kronick 1985 version, hedged: "It has been suggested that spinach got its reputation as a dietary supplement because of a misplaced decimal point in which the iron value was given as ten times higher than it was."
- Larsson 1995, J Intern Med 238(5):445-450, titled "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation", repeats the story citing Hamblin. Rekdal: "Larsson obviously has an important message, but he manages to do exactly what he is criticizing others for in quite a spectacular way."
- Dates disagree across tellings. Rekdal: "a large number of apparently independent sources have mutually confirmed the 'fact' that a decimal point error was made, whether it was in 1870, in the 1890s, or in the 1930s."
- The "doing the opposite of what they are preaching" quote above.
- Rekdal opens his paper by citing Larsson himself, then admits: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson." (deliberate demonstration)
- Rekdal gives no count of citing papers. Do not invent one.
