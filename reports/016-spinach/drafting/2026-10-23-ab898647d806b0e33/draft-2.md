## The incident

In the BMJ's 1981 Christmas issue, T.J. Hamblin published "Fake!", a survey of "Frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes". 1 paragraph covered spinach: "German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value." It closed: "For a source of iron Popeye would have been better off chewing the cans."

The paragraph carries no citation. The article ends with 13 publications it was "derived from". None is about spinach.

Raw spinach holds 2.71 mg of iron per 100 g, by the USDA's count. Sutton cites a 1938 measurement of dried spinach at 44.8 mg. A 2009 textbook by Coultate, as Sutton quotes it, offers 2 versions: von Bunge found 35 mg per 100 g in 1890 but had analysed dried spinach, or von Wolf misplaced the decimal point about 20 years earlier. The famous figure may be a correct measurement of a different thing.

## Who lied

Nobody, on the record available. The earliest version Rekdal found is Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, London: "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point." Kronick in 1985 also hedged: "It has been suggested." Hamblin's version dropped the hedge and added the German chemists.

Sutton, a Reader in Criminology at Nottingham Trent University, emailed Hamblin. Sutton reports that Hamblin "honestly cannot remember", that the BMJ's then editor had asked him for a humorous Christmas piece and asked him "not to provide references", and that Hamblin was "sure that he had not made it up."

Sutton's questions included "Who is von Wolff and where are his erroneous findings recorded?" After weeks of searching he could not answer it. He also read the Thimble Theatre strips from 1928 to 1935. In the strip of 3 July 1932, the first in which Popeye explains his spinach, he credits vitamin A. Sutton's conclusion: "Segar chose and promoted spinach for its vitamin A content alone."

The tellings also disagree on the year. Rekdal: sources had "mutually confirmed the 'fact' that a decimal point error was made, whether it was in 1870, in the 1890s, or in the 1930s."

## The bit continues

In 1995 the Journal of Internal Medicine published Larsson's "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation". It repeated the spinach story, citing Hamblin. Rekdal: "Larsson obviously has an important message, but he manages to do exactly what he is criticizing others for in quite a spectacular way."

Rekdal's own 2014 paper, "Academic urban legends", opens by citing Larsson for the story, then stops: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson." The demonstration was deliberate.
