VERIFIED FACTS (use only these; each with its source):
A. Vreeman RC, Carroll AE, "Medical myths", BMJ 2007;335:1288-1289 (published 22 Dec 2007), full text https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2151163/ . Verbatim: "The advice to drink at least eight glasses of water a day can be found throughout the popular press." "One origin may be a 1945 recommendation that stated: A suitable allowance of water for adults is 2.5 litres daily in most instances. An ordinary standard for diverse persons is 1 millilitre for each calorie of food. Most of this quantity is contained in prepared foods." "If the last, crucial sentence is ignored, the statement could be interpreted as instruction to drink eight glasses of water a day." "Another endorsement may have come from a prominent nutritionist, Frederick Stare, who once recommended, without references, the consumption 'around 6 to 8 glasses per 24 hours,' which could be 'in the form of coffee, tea, milk, soft drinks, beer, etc.'" "The complete lack of evidence supporting the recommendation to drink six to eight glasses of water a day is exhaustively catalogued in an invited review by Heinz Valtin in the American Journal of Physiology." "In contrast, drinking excess amounts of water can be dangerous, resulting in water intoxication, hyponatraemia, and even death." It was 1 of 7 myths they selected; they noted where there was "no evidence to support the claim".
B. Valtin H, "'Drink at least eight glasses of water a day.' Really? Is there scientific evidence for '8 x 8'?", Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol 2002;283:R993-R1004. Abstract, verbatim (PubMed 12376390, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12376390/): the admonition comes "with an accompanying reminder that beverages containing caffeine and alcohol do not count"; the search covered electronic databases, "a cursory examination of the older literature", and "extensive consultation with several nutritionists who specialize in the field of thirst and drinking fluids". "No scientific studies were found in support of 8 x 8." Surveys of "thousands of adults" suggest "such large amounts are not needed". Published studies show "caffeinated drinks (and, to a lesser extent, mild alcoholic beverages like beer in moderation) may indeed be counted toward the daily total". Scope: "healthy adults in a temperate climate leading a largely sedentary existence". Caveat: "large intakes of fluid, equal to and greater than 8 x 8, are advisable for the treatment or prevention of some diseases and certainly are called for under special circumstances, such as vigorous work and exercise, especially in hot climates." "Since it is difficult or impossible to prove a negative... the author invites communications from readers who are aware of pertinent publications."
C. Attribution of the 1945 line to the US Food and Nutrition Board: The Physiologist (American Physiological Society magazine), "The Science of Hydration", Marijke Vroomen Durning, July 2021, https://www.physiology.org/publications/news/the-physiologist-magazine/2021/july/the-science-of-hydration : "In 1945, the U.S. Food and Nutrition Board issued guidelines indicating that people should drink two and a half liters of fluid per day. However, there was an added bit of information associated with the recommendation that seems to have been lost over the years, as if people stopped reading before they got to the end".
D. National Academies news release, 11 Feb 2004, on the Institute of Medicine's Dietary Reference Intakes for water, https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/report-sets-dietary-intake-levels-for-water-salt-and-potassium-to-maintain-health-and-reduce-chronic-disease-risk . Verbatim: "The vast majority of healthy people adequately meet their daily hydration needs by letting thirst be their guide". Women "approximately 2.7 liters (91 ounces) of total water", men "approximately 3.7 liters (125 ounces)". "About 80 percent of people's total water comes from drinking water and beverages -- including caffeinated beverages -- and the other 20 percent is derived from food." "We don't offer any rule of thumb based on how many glasses of water people should drink each day because our hydration needs can be met through a variety of sources in addition to drinking water," said Lawrence Appel, chair of the panel that wrote the report.
E. IOM report itself, chapter 4 "Water", https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6 : "The AI for total water intake for young men and women (19 to 30 years) is 3.7 L ... and 2.7 L .../day, respectively, which correspond to median intakes for this age group in the NHANES III survey". "It would be misinterpreting the basis for setting the AI to state that there is a 'requirement' for water at the level of the AI." "there is no single daily total water requirement for a given person". The chapter's reference list cites Valtin 2002.
F. NHS (UK), "Water, drinks and nutrition", page last reviewed 17 May 2023, https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/food-guidelines-and-food-labels/water-drinks-nutrition/ : "In the Eatwell Guide, the government recommends that people should aim to drink 6 to 8 cups or glasses of fluid a day. This is just a guide". "Water, lower-fat milk and lower-sugar drinks, including tea and coffee, all count as part of your daily intake."
G. Arithmetic: 8 glasses x 8 oz = 64 oz, about 1.9 L (1 L = 33.8 fl oz, per the IOM report's conversion factor in E). 2.5 L is about 10.6 8-oz glasses.

Stare's book was not seen; his line is as quoted in the BMJ. HOUSE NOTES: The shape: everyone assumes 8x8 has a study behind it; the 2 searches that looked found none, and the most likely ancestor says most of the water is in food. The rule even acquired an unsourced amendment (caffeine doesn't count) that Valtin found studies contradicting. Who lied: nobody; a sentence was dropped, per the papers' own hedged account. The fix: Valtin 2002, IOM 2004 ("no rule of thumb", thirst), BMJ 2007. The bit continues: NHS 2023 still says 6 to 8 cups or glasses, now counting tea and coffee, "just a guide". Do NOT frame the NHS as wrong; state what the page says, flatly. Hostile-read caution: never imply drinking water is bad or that people should drink less; disclosure should carry a health note like 007's, including Valtin's caveat (exercise, heat, some diseases) and the BMJ's hyponatraemia line.
