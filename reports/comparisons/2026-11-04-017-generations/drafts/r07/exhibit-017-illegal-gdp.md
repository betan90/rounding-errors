## The incident

The European System of National Accounts 1995 required member states to include illegal activities in GDP: consensual transactions, whatever their legal status, belong in the production boundary. In 2012 the EU directed all members to address "areas for improvement" by 2014. ONS published estimates for prostitution and drugs in May 2014. Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew described the method.

Prostitution required counting what nobody counts. Eaves, a London charity, surveyed off-street workers in 2004: approximately 7,000. The Metropolitan Police estimated 115 at any one time. Scaled to the UK: roughly 58,000 prostitutes nationally.

ONS set client frequency at 20–30 per worker per week, following Netherlands data. Price: £55 in 2004, derived from an online source, varied by CPI. Intermediate costs: approximately 125 annually on clothes, 0.50 per client on condoms.

The method flagged its constraints. The prostitute count was weak. ONS lacked a time series and so varied the count by male population growth: "necessary because we have no time series data." For drugs: "We assume that the purity-adjusted amount of drugs consumed by the average user remains constant over time. This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." Cannabis split: "an arbitrary assumption". Government spending: "government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs".

The prostitution component: 0.35% of GDP. Drugs: 0.24%. Total illegal activity: 0.58%.

By 2015, ONS's FOI response set 2010 prostitution at £5.6 billion. Today's published series puts 2010 at £4,370 million. In 2016 ONS applied a refinement: Statistics Netherlands estimated prostitutes take 12 weeks' holiday per year, and ONS incorporated this into the UK estimates.

## Who lied

ONS did not hide the method. Every assumption and its limitations appear in the published article. A footnote cites Wikipedia for client pricing. The prostitute estimate comes from a London charity survey and police observation, scaled upward. The method is revisited at each accounts release, and flagged assumptions are carried forward in every publication.

The regulations required inclusion. The weakness is not concealment: it is that GDP now carries a component built almost entirely from flagged assumptions.

## The bit continues

The series continues as published. The latest release, 31 October 2025, carries estimates from 2014 forward. The 2020 figure fell sharply to £4,130 million, down 31% from 2019. The 2022 estimate recovered to £6,142 million. No ONS source explains the 2020 movement. The estimates remain modelled with the published assumptions in place.
