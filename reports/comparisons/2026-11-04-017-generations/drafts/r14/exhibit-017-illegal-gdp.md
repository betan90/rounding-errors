## The incident

A 2012 EU audit required Member States to address illegal activities by 2014. The European System of Accounts 1995 section 1.13 states that illegal transactions to which all units involved consent are included within the production boundary. GNI is used to calculate EU budget contributions. Comparability required all countries to include it.

In May 2014 ONS published its method for including illegal drugs and prostitution in UK GDP. The executive summary noted "significant limitations in the availability of data" and that the estimates were "partly based" on assumptions, "especially" for prostitution. The article stated "there is no time series" for the number of prostitutes.

The method: multiply prostitutes by clients per week by 52. Number of prostitutes: a 2004 charity called advertised numbers and asked how many prostitutes were at each location, finding 7,000 off-street in London. The Metropolitan Police counted 115 on-street. Scaled to the UK: c. 58,000. Clients per week: "Following a Netherlands assumption", ONS calculated estimates based on 20, 25 and 30. Price: £55 in 2004, from Punternet. Footnote 12 cited Wikipedia.

Because there was no time series, ONS varied the point estimate by the male population. Assumption 1: "the number of prostitutes has the same pattern through time as the 16+ male population. This is a weak assumption."

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS published a table of assumptions. 3 are reproduced verbatim here.

"We assume that the purity-adjusted amount of drugs consumed by the average user remains constant over time. This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it."

"We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption."

"The government final consumption expenditure assumption is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

The word "arbitrary" appears twice in the article; "probably false" appears 3 times.

In October 2014 the ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser published "Sex, Drugs and GDP" on the Civil Service Quarterly blog. "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!) into the numbers of prostitutes and the price of drugs yielded the result that spending on these illegal activities in the UK contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP." The italics were his.

## The bit continues

An FOI response in November 2015 gave the 2010 prostitution figure as £5.6bn. The Blue Book 2016 revisions article described a refinement: "allowance was made to reduce the estimates made for prostitution in household final consumption expenditure by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes. Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

The time series MNC8, last released 31 October 2025, gives prostitution in 2010 as £4,370m. The series runs from 1985 to 2024. In 2019 it stood at £5,993m. In 2020 it fell 31% to £4,130m. By 2022 it had recovered to £6,142m. The method says there is no time series. The chart plots the assumptions.
