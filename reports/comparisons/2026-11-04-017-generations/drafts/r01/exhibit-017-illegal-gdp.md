## The incident

The European System of National Accounts, ESA95, requires that illegal transactions "to which all units involved consent" be included in GDP. Section 1.13 applies across Europe; member states' gross national income is used to calculate EU budget contributions. In 2012 the EU audited national accounting methods across all members. By 2014 member states had to implement improvements.

In May 2014 ONS published its method. For prostitution: P × C × 52. P is the number of prostitutes. C is weekly clients per prostitute. 52 is weeks per year. The Eaves charity estimated approximately 7,000 off-street prostitutes in London in 2004 by telephoning advertisements; adding Metropolitan Police street counts gave roughly 58,000 for the whole country. Clients per week came from "a Netherlands assumption" of 20, 25, or 30 per week (which figure was chosen is not stated). Price was £55 in 2004, sourced from Punternet; a footnote references Wikipedia.

Four assumptions are listed. One: "the number of prostitutes has the same pattern through time as the 16+ male population. This is a weak assumption." For drugs, the article states: "We assume that the purity-adjusted amount of drugs consumed by the average user remains constant over time. This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." Also: "We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption." The words "probably false" appear 3 times in the assumptions table.

ONS publishes the series from 1985 onwards. No direct observations exist for 1985 to 2003. The series is calculated backward by scaling the 2004 point estimate against population growth and price inflation. In 2020 the series fell 31%, from £5,993m in 2019 to £4,130m. ONS has published no explanation for this movement.

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS published the method as a peer-reviewed article, naming the assumptions and flagging their limitations.

## The bit continues

In 2016 ONS refined the estimate. It reduced prostitution expenditure by applying a factor for time off work. Statistics Netherlands had estimated that sex workers take 12 weeks of holiday per year; ONS applied the same adjustment to UK figures.

The effect appears in 2010 data. An ONS FOI response from November 2015 stated that prostitution was £5.6bn in 2010. The current MNC8 series lists 2010 as £4,370m. That ratio, 0.78, matches the working-week ratio: 40/52 = 0.77. The change aligns with the holiday factor, though ONS has not confirmed this accounts for the entire difference.

The series is still published. The last release was 31 October 2025.
