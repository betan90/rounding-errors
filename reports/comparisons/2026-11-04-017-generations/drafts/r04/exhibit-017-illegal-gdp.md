## The incident

ONS's 2014 article explains why it began including illegal drugs and prostitution in the UK national accounts. Gross national income "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget", and a 2012 audit had identified "areas for improvement". The rule was already there: illegal transactions "to which all units involved consent are included within the production boundary".

The article says its data have "significant limitations" and that its estimates "are, therefore, partly based" on assumptions. "This applies especially to the estimates of prostitution activity."

For prostitution, clients are estimated at "20, 25 and 30" a week, following a Netherlands assumption. Eaves, a charity, estimated about 7,000 off-street prostitutes in London by calling numbers advertised in the media. The Metropolitan Police counted 115 on the street at any given time. Scaling those figures up by population gives "c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country." ONS's method has "no time series" for the number of prostitutes, so the count is varied through time by the male population. The article calls that "a weak assumption."

The price, "£55 in 2004", comes from "an estimate from Punternet". ONS footnotes Punternet to its Wikipedia page.

Drug consumption per user is held constant over time. "This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." Half of cannabis is assumed to be imported and half home-grown. "This is an arbitrary assumption." The government spending assumption is called reasonable because "government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

In a 2014 blog post, ONS deputy chief economic adviser Peter Patterson wrote that spending on drugs and prostitution "contributes up to £10 billion a year" to GDP, and that the overall effect was to raise GDP "by around £50 billion a year."

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS published each assumption, labelled the weak ones, and footnoted its price source. The totals reached readers through Patterson's blog post. The article itself "does not include numerical impacts."

## The bit continues

Blue Book 2016 added a refinement. ONS reduced the prostitution estimates "to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes", using a factor from Statistics Netherlands: "12 weeks per year", applied to the UK estimates.

The FOI reply gave 2010 prostitution spending as £5.6bn in current prices, 0.4% of GDP. The current MNC8 series gives £4,370m for 2010. This site's arithmetic, not ONS's: £4,370m is 78% of £5.6bn, and 40 of 52 weeks is 77%. ONS does not state that the holiday factor is the whole difference.

The series runs from 1985 to 2024 and is modelled, not observed. ONS last released it on 31 October 2025. It falls from 5,993 in 2019 to 4,130 in 2020, then rises to 6,142 in 2022. This site has not found an explanation for the fall.
