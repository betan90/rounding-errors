## The incident

The European System of National Accounts 1995 (ESA 1995) requires member states to include illegal activities in GDP. Section 1.13: "illegal transactions to which all units involved consent are included within the production boundary." ONS published a method in 2014. The authors, Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew, wrote that the change addressed a GNI reservation, and that GNI "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget."

The method for prostitution starts with a 2004 estimate from Eaves, a charity, "that there were approx 7000 off-street prostitutes in London in that year; this was done by calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place." The Metropolitan Police estimated 115 prostitutes on the street at any one time in London. "Adding these and scaling up by UK population ... gives c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country."

Clients per week came from a Netherlands assumption. Price came from Punternet, "an estimate from Punternet supported by a 2004 journal article. This is 55 in 2004." Footnote 12 reads: "http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punternet".

The article's first assumption for prostitution: "the number of prostitutes has the same pattern through time as the 16+ male population. This is a weak assumption ... It is necessary because we have no time series data for the number of prostitutes."

The drugs assumptions include: "We assume that the purity-adjusted amount of drugs consumed by the average user remains constant over time. This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." Also: "We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption." One more: "We assume that no drugs other than cannabis are produced in the UK. While this is probably false, we do not have any evidence to the contrary." The phrase "probably false" appears 3 times in the article; "arbitrary" appears 2 times.

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS published its method, named its sources, and flagged its weaknesses. The authors wrote: "significant limitations in the availability of data ... requires a number of assumptions on which the estimates are, therefore, partly based. This applies especially to the estimates of prostitution activity."

## The bit continues

In Blue Book 2016 the series was revised. ONS: "allowance was made to reduce the estimates made for prostitution in household final consumption expenditure by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes. Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

The November 2015 FOI response gave the 2010 figure as 5.6 billion. Today's published series gives 4,370 million for 2010. The series peaked at 5,993 million in 2019, fell to 4,130 million in 2020, and recovered to 6,431 million in 2024. The latest release was 31 October 2025.
