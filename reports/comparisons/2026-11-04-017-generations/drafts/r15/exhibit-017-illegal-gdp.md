## The incident

The UK's national accounts count spending on illegal drugs and prostitution as part of GDP. The rule is old. ONS's 2014 article quotes ESA95 section 1.13, which says illegal transactions "to which all units involved consent are included within the production boundary". Its footnote adds: "There are no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA10." The stated reason is money. GNI "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget."

The prostitution estimate starts with headcounts. For London in 2004, a charity called advertised numbers and estimated 7,000 off-street prostitutes. The Metropolitan Police counted 115 on the street. Scaled by UK population, the total is "c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country." The article admits "there is no time series", so it varies its point estimates by the male population, which it calls "a weak assumption". Clients are taken as 20, 25 and 30 a week, following "a Netherlands assumption". The price, £55 in 2004, comes from a Punternet estimate, footnoted to a Wikipedia page.

The drug assumptions are labelled the same way. Half of cannabis is assumed imported and half home-grown: "This is an arbitrary assumption." Drug purity is assumed constant: "This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it."

## Who lied

Nobody lied. ONS published each assumption and labelled the weak ones. The 2014 article is by Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew. It "does not include numerical impacts".

The numbers came later, in a blog post on the Civil Service Quarterly blog by Peter Patterson, ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser. Patterson wrote that the overall effect was to raise GDP "by around £50 billion a year", and that the inclusion is "legally mandated through the European System of Accounts 2010".

## The bit continues

The series is still published. Time series MNC8, "12.2 Prostitution", runs from 1985 to 2024 in £m. The chart plots modelled values, not observed ones. It falls from 5,993 in 2019 to 4,130 in 2020, then reaches 6,142 in 2022. This site found no ONS note explaining the 2020 fall.

In the Blue Book 2016 revisions, ONS reduced the prostitution estimates "by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes". Statistics Netherlands had estimated "12 weeks per year", and "the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

Both 2010 figures are ONS's. An ONS FOI reply gives prostitution at £5.6bn for 2010. The current series gives £4,370m for the same year. This site's arithmetic, not ONS's: £4,370m is 78% of £5.6bn, and 52 weeks less 12 leaves 40, or 77%. ONS does not say the holiday is the whole difference.
