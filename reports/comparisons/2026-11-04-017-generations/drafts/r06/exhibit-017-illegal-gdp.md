## The incident

In May 2014 the Office for National Statistics began counting illegal drugs and prostitution in UK GDP. The rule was not new. The European System of National Accounts 1995, section 1.13, puts illegal transactions "to which all units involved consent" inside the production boundary. GNI "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget", and in 2012 an EU audit identified areas "which Member States have to address by 2014".

The data did not exist. ONS said so: "significant limitations in the availability of data ... requires a number of assumptions on which the estimates are, therefore, partly based. This applies especially to the estimates of prostitution activity."

So the assumptions were the data. Eaves, a charity, estimated roughly 7,000 off-street prostitutes in London in 2004, "done by calling numbers advertised in various media". The Metropolitan Police gave "115 prostitutes on the street at any one time". "Adding these and scaling up by UK population ... gives c. 58,000 prostitutes." The price, £55 in 2004, came from Punternet, footnoted in the article to a Wikipedia page. Clients per week followed "a Netherlands assumption".

## Who lied

Nobody. Every assumption is in the published article, labelled. "probably false" appears 3 times; "arbitrary" twice. On cannabis: "We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption." On public spending: "The government final consumption expenditure assumption is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs." On the labour side: "There is no employment in prostitution; all prostitutes and pimps are self-employed. This is a controversial assumption."

Peter Patterson, then ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote on a government blog that "ONS desk research ... yielded the result that spending on these illegal activities in the UK contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP." The FOI reply put N2, drugs and prostitution together, at 0.58% of 2010 GDP.

## The bit continues

In Blue Book 2016 ONS refined the model. "allowance was made to reduce the estimates made for prostitution in household final consumption expenditure by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes. Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

The refinement moved the number. The 2015 FOI reply gave prostitution in 2010 as £5.6bn. Today's MNC8 series gives 2010 as £4,370m. 4,370 divided by 5,600 is 0.78; 40 of 52 working weeks is 0.77. That arithmetic is this site's. ONS does not say the holiday is the whole difference.

The series runs from 1985. The first estimate was published in 2014. It still publishes.
