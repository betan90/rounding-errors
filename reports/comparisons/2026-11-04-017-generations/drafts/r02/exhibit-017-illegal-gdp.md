## The incident

In 2012 the European Commission audited how member states calculated Gross National Income. GNI determines each country's contribution to the EU budget. The audit identified areas for improvement, to be addressed by 2014. One: the European System of Accounts required member states to include illegal activities in GDP when "all units involved consent". Prostitution and drugs qualified.

ONS published its method in May 2014. Authors: Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew. The executive summary: "significant limitations in the availability of data ... requires a number of assumptions on which the estimates are, therefore, partly based. This applies especially to the estimates of prostitution activity."

The method for prostitution: P times C times 52, where P is the number of prostitutes and C is clients per week. P: a 2004 Eaves charity estimate of 7,000 off-street prostitutes in London, derived by "calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place"; a Metropolitan Police count of 115 on the street at any one time; the sum scaled by population to c. 58,000 across the UK. C: "Following a Netherlands assumption with some research support", 20, 25 or 30. Price: £55 in 2004, from "an estimate from Punternet supported by a 2004 journal article", varied by the consumer price index for personal services. Footnote 12 for Punternet: "http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punternet".

Time series: none. Assumption 1: "the number of prostitutes has the same pattern through time as the 16+ male population. This is a weak assumption ... It is necessary because we have no time series data for the number of prostitutes."

## Who lied

Nobody. The article lists its assumptions in Table 3. On drugs: "We assume that the purity-adjusted amount of drugs consumed by the average user remains constant over time. This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." On cannabis: "We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption." On government consumption: "The government final consumption expenditure assumption is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

"Probably false" appears 3 times in the article. "Arbitrary" appears twice.

The employment assumption: "There is no employment in prostitution; all prostitutes and pimps are self-employed. This is a controversial assumption."

Patterson, ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, in October 2014: "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!) into the numbers of prostitutes and the price of drugs yielded the result that spending on these illegal activities in the UK contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP."

## The bit continues

In 2016 ONS published a refinement. Blue Book 2016's revisions article: "As a further refinement, allowance was made to reduce the estimates made for prostitution in household final consumption expenditure by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes. Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

An FOI response from November 2015 gave prostitution in 2010 as £5.6bn. The MNC8 series, last released 31 October 2025, gives 2010 as £4,370m. The ratio is 0.78. 40 working weeks of 52 is 0.77.

The series begins in 1985, 29 years before ONS published the first estimate. 2019: £5,993m. 2020: £4,130m, down 31%. 2022: £6,142m. The series is still published. The chart plots the assumptions.
