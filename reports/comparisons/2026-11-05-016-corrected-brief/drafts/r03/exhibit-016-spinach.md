## The incident

In its Christmas issue of 19 December 1981, the BMJ published "Fake!" by T.J. Hamblin, an article about "Frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes". A paragraph covered spinach: "German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value." For iron, it added, Popeye "would have been better off chewing the cans."

The paragraph has no citation. The article ends with 13 publications Hamblin would "particularly like to mention". In 2010 Mike Sutton, Reader in Criminology at Nottingham Trent University, counted the same 13: "none of which, however, cite the source of the decimal error or 1930's Germans".

## Who lied

Nobody, on the record. The story predates the article. Rekdal (2014) reports a 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, University of London, in which Bender said "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point." Bender hedged. Kronick, in 1985, also hedged: "It has been suggested." Hamblin stated it as fact.

Sutton emailed Hamblin. Sutton reports that Hamblin "honestly cannot remember", that the BMJ's then editor had asked him for a humorous Christmas piece and "asked not to provide references", and that he "was sure that he had not made it up."

The tellings do not agree on when the error happened. Rekdal: sources "mutually confirmed the 'fact' that a decimal point error was made, whether it was in 1870, in the 1890s, or in the 1930s." Sutton asked: "Who is von Wolff and where are his erroneous findings recorded?" His references list von Wolff's 1871 Aschen-analysen. No source here produces the page with the misplaced decimal point.

Sutton quotes a second version, from a 2009 Royal Society of Chemistry book by Coultate: von Bunge in 1890 found 35 mg of iron per 100 g, but had analysed dried spinach. Dried spinach has since measured 44.8 mg (Jackson 1938) and about 34 mg (Oladele and Obarisaide 2009). USDA lists raw spinach at 2.71 mg. The high figure may be a correct measurement of a different food.

Also: Popeye. Sutton read the Thimble Theatre strips from 1928 to 1935. In the strip of 3 July 1932, the first in which Popeye explains his spinach, the reason is vitamin A. In "hundreds of Popeye cartoons studied", Sutton found none linking spinach to iron.

## The bit continues

In 1995 Larsson published "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation" in the Journal of Internal Medicine. It repeated the spinach story, citing Hamblin. Rekdal: "Larsson obviously has an important message, but he manages to do exactly what he is criticizing others for in quite a spectacular way."

Rekdal opens his own paper by citing Larsson for the story. Later he discloses the method: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson." It was a demonstration.
