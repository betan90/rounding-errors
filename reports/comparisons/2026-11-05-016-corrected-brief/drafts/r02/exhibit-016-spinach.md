## The incident

In December 1981 the BMJ's Christmas issue carried T. J. Hamblin's "Fake!", an article on "frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes". It said spinach's reputation as an iron source rested on an error. The original workers, in the 1890s, put the decimal point in the wrong place, and German chemists in the 1930s found a 10-fold overestimate. The paragraph has no citation. The article ends with 13 references. Sutton counts none of them as citing the decimal error or the 1930s Germans.

The story predates him. In a 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, University of London, Bender wrote that "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point." Rekdal finds "a large number of apparently independent sources" that have "mutually confirmed" the error, whether it was made "in 1870, in the 1890s, or in the 1930s." Larsson's 1995 paper repeated it, citing Hamblin.

## Who lied

The record is hedges, omissions and repetition. Bender hedged. Hamblin stated the figure without a source. Sutton reports Hamblin's reply to his email. Hamblin wrote that he had been asked about this before and that he "honestly cannot remember" it. He said the BMJ's editor had asked him for a humorous Christmas piece and had asked him not to provide references. He was, Sutton reports, "sure that he had not made it up."

## The bit continues

Sutton read the bound volumes of the Thimble Theatre strips from 1928 to 1935. The strip of 3 July 1932, the first in which Popeye explains why he eats spinach, credits vitamin A. Of the "hundreds of Popeye cartoons" he studied, none links spinach to iron. "In fact, Segar chose and promoted spinach for its vitamin A content alone."

The measurement is disputed as well. An account Sutton quotes says the high figure came from dried spinach. He cites dried-spinach measurements of 44.8 mg and about 34 mg per 100 g. If that account is right, the figure measured something other than fresh spinach.

The story is offered as the classic example of an uncorrected error, and it has no traceable source. Several of the people who retold it were writing about accuracy and citation. Rekdal's line on them: "By using the non-existent, or at least undocumented, decimal point error as an illustration of the importance of accuracy and critical investigation, Bender, Hamblin, Larsson, and Skrabanek and McCormick all end up doing the opposite of what they are preaching."
