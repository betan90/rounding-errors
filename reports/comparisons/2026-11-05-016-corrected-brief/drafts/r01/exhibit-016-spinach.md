## The incident

Spinach is rich in iron, the story goes, because somebody put a decimal point in the wrong place. Hamblin's 1981 article in the BMJ's Christmas issue states it as settled. He dates the original measurement to the 1890s and says German chemists showed in the 1930s that the original workers had overstated its iron by a factor of 10. "Spinach is no better for you than cabbage, Brussels sprouts, or broccoli," he concludes.

Rekdal finds the claim earlier, in a 1972 inaugural lecture by Bender at Queen Elizabeth College, University of London.

## Who lied

Bender hedged. The fame of spinach, he said, "may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point."

Hamblin stated the German correction without a source. His passage carries no citation, and Sutton counts 13 references in the whole article, none of which cites the decimal error or the 1930s Germans.

Sutton reports that Hamblin, in an email, said he had been asked by the BMJ's editor for a humorous Christmas piece, and had been asked not to provide references. Hamblin, Sutton reports, closed by saying he was "sure that he had not made it up."

## The bit continues

Sutton read the Thimble Theatre strips from 1928 to 1935. In the strip of 3 July 1932, the first in which Popeye explains why he eats spinach, the credit goes to vitamin A. "Segar chose and promoted spinach for its vitamin A content alone," Sutton writes.

Sutton also quotes a 2009 chemistry book on the 1890 measurement, which was made on dried spinach: "Wherever lies the truth, the error was not picked up until 1937."

The textbook example of an uncorrected error has no traceable source. It was retold by writers warning about bad citation. Larsson's 1995 paper, "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation," repeated the story and cited Hamblin.
