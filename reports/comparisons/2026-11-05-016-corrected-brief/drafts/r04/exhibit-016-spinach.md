## The incident

The BMJ's Christmas issue of 19 December 1981 carried "Fake!", an article by T.J. Hamblin on "Frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes". A single paragraph covered spinach. The discovery that it was "as valuable a source of iron as red meat" was made in the 1890s, he wrote, but "German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value." His conclusion: "For a source of iron Popeye would have been better off chewing the cans."

The paragraph has no citation. The article ends with 13 publications Hamblin says he would "particularly like to mention". In 2010 Sutton, a Reader in Criminology at Nottingham Trent University, counted them: "none of which, however, cite the source of the decimal error or 1930's Germans".

## Who lied

Nobody, on the record. The story is older than Hamblin's paragraph. In a 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, University of London, Bender said "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point". A 1985 version by Kronick kept the hedge: "It has been suggested." Hamblin's version stated it as fact.

Sutton emailed Hamblin. Sutton reports that Hamblin "honestly cannot remember" the source, that the BMJ's then editor had asked him for "a humorous piece" and asked him "not to provide references", and that Hamblin "was sure that he had not made it up."

Sutton also quotes a 2009 Royal Society of Chemistry book by Coultate, which offers 2 versions. Either von Bunge found 35 mg of iron per 100 g in 1890 but had analysed dried spinach, or a chemist about 20 years earlier misplaced the decimal point. "Wherever lies the truth, the error was not picked up until 1937". Raw spinach, per the USDA: 2.71 mg per 100 g.

Sutton read the Thimble Theatre strips from 1928 to 1935. He reports that the strip of 3 July 1932, the first in which Popeye explains why he eats spinach, credits vitamin A, and that none of the "hundreds of Popeye cartoons studied" links spinach to iron.

## The bit continues

In 1995 Larsson published "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation" in the Journal of Internal Medicine. It repeats the spinach story, citing Hamblin. Rekdal (2014): "Larsson obviously has an important message, but he manages to do exactly what he is criticizing others for in quite a spectacular way."

Rekdal opens his own paper by citing Larsson, then explains, as a deliberate demonstration: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson."

The tellings do not agree on when the error happened. Rekdal: "a large number of apparently independent sources have mutually confirmed the 'fact' that a decimal point error was made, whether it was in 1870, in the 1890s, or in the 1930s."
