## The incident

In May 2014 the Office for National Statistics (ONS) published how it would add illegal drugs and prostitution to the UK national accounts. The article, by Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew, gave the reason. Gross national income "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget", and a 2012 audit of EU methods had found "a number of areas for improvement" to address by 2014. The rule was older: ESA95 section 1.13 counts illegal transactions "to which all units involved consent". ONS's footnote: "There are no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA10."

The data, the article said, had "significant limitations", which "requires a number of assumptions on which the estimates are, therefore, partly based. This applies especially to the estimates of prostitution activity."

The prostitution method multiplied prostitutes, clients and price by 52. In 2004 the charity Eaves "estimated that there were approx 7000 off-street prostitutes in London in that year; this was done by calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". The Metropolitan Police counted "115 prostitutes on the street at any one time in London". Scaled up by UK population: "c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country." Clients per week followed "a Netherlands assumption with some research support": estimates on 20, 25 and 30. The price, £55 in 2004, came from Punternet. Punternet's footnote is a Wikipedia URL.

"As there is no time series", the 2004 figures were moved with the 16+ male population. ONS: "This is a weak assumption."

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS published every assumption and graded it.

"We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption."

"We assume that the purity-adjusted amount of drugs consumed by the average user remains constant over time. This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." The phrase "probably false" appears 3 times in the article.

Government spending on drugs was set aside "because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

"There is no employment in prostitution; all prostitutes and pimps are self-employed. This is a controversial assumption".

## The bit continues

An ONS freedom-of-information reply put illegal production in 2010 at £9,097m, 0.58% of GDP. It gave prostitution spending for 2010 as £5.6bn.

In Blue Book 2016, ONS reduced the prostitution estimates "by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes. Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

Series MNC8 now gives 2010 as £4,370m. By this site's arithmetic, not ONS's, £4,370m is 78% of £5.6bn, and a 40-week year is 77% of 52 weeks. ONS does not say the holiday is the whole difference.

The series begins in 1985, 29 years before ONS's first estimate.
