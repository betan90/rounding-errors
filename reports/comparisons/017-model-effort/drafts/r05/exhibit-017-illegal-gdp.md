## The incident

Gross national income "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget". In 2012 an audit across EU countries found "a number of areas for improvement", due by 2014. The rule dated from ESA95, section 1.13: "illegal transactions to which all units involved consent are included within the production boundary". ONS notes "There are no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA10."

On 29 May 2014 ONS published its method, in an article by Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew. It warns of "significant limitations in the availability of data", so the estimates are partly based on assumptions. "This applies especially to the estimates of prostitution activity."

The count starts in London in 2004. Eaves, a charity, estimated approx 7,000 off-street prostitutes, "by calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". The Metropolitan Police reported 115 on the street at any one time. Scaled up by UK population: "c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country."

Clients per week follow "a Netherlands assumption with some research support"; ONS calculates at 20, 25 and 30. The price, £55 in 2004, is "an estimate from Punternet supported by a 2004 journal article". The footnote for Punternet is a Wikipedia page.

Other years are modelled: "As there is no time series, we vary the point estimates in time using the male population". ONS on that: "This is a weak assumption".

On cannabis: "We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption." On other drugs made in the UK: "While this is probably false, we do not have any evidence to the contrary." On government spending: "government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS published every assumption alongside its own assessment of it.

In October 2014 Peter Patterson, ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote in Civil Service Quarterly that "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!) into the numbers of prostitutes and the price of drugs" found these activities contribute "up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP". He added: "Comparability between nations dictates that all countries include it in their GDP figures."

## The bit continues

An ONS Freedom of Information reply put prostitution spending in 2010 at "£5.6bn (current prices), which is 0.4% of GDP".

Blue Book 2016 added "a further refinement": a reduction "to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes. Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

The series, MNC8, now runs from 1985, 29 years before the first estimate was published. Its value for 2010 is £4,370m. This site's arithmetic, not ONS's: £4,370m is 78% of £5.6bn, and 40 working weeks are 77% of 52. ONS does not say the holiday accounts for the whole difference.
