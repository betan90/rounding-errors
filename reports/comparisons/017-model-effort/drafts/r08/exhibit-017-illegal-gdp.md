## The incident

National accounts rules have long counted illegal transactions to which all parties consent. ESA95 said so in section 1.13. In 2014 the UK had to act on it: GNI "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget", and a 2012 audit gave Member States until 2014 to fix the gaps.

On 29 May 2014 ONS published the method, by Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew. It warned of "significant limitations in the availability of data". Prostitution spending is P*C*52: prostitutes, times clients, times weeks.

The prostitutes came from Eaves, a charity, which in 2004 "estimated that there were approx 7000 off-street prostitutes in London in that year; this was done by calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". The Metropolitan Police added 115 on the street. Scaled by UK population: "c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country."

Clients per week followed "a Netherlands assumption with some research support": 20, 25 and 30. The price, £55 in 2004, came from Punternet. Footnote 12 for Punternet is a Wikipedia page.

"As there is no time series, we vary the point estimates in time using the male population." ONS: "This is a weak assumption."

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS published every assumption and graded most of them itself.

On drugs: "We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption." On consumption per user: "This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." On domestic production: "While this is probably false, we do not have any evidence to the contrary." On self-employment of all prostitutes: "This is a controversial assumption".

1 assumption ONS called reasonable, "because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

Peter Patterson, ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote on the Civil Service Quarterly blog that "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!)" put the activities at up to £10 billion a year of GDP.

## The bit continues

An ONS FOI reply gave 2010 prostitution spending as £5.6bn. Blue Book 2016 then reduced the estimates "by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes". The factor came from abroad: "Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

Series MNC8 now gives 2010 as £4,370m. That is 78% of £5.6bn; 40 working weeks are 77% of 52. The ratio is this site's arithmetic. ONS does not say the holiday accounts for the whole difference.

The series starts in 1985, 29 years before the first estimate. It reached £5,993m in 2019, fell to £4,130m in 2020, and stood at £6,142m in 2022. ONS last released it on 31 October 2025.
