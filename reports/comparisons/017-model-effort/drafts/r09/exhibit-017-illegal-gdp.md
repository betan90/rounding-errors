## The incident

On 29 May 2014 ONS published how illegal drugs and prostitution would enter the UK national accounts. Its article notes that the European System of National Accounts 1995 "states that illegal transactions to which all units involved consent are included within the production boundary". Gross national income, it adds, "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget". A 2012 audit of methods across EU countries had found "a number of areas for improvement", to be addressed by 2014.

For prostitution, annual volume is "P\*C\*52": prostitutes, times clients a week, times 52 weeks. P starts with a 2004 estimate by Eaves, a charity: "approx 7000 off-street prostitutes in London"; "this was done by calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". The Metropolitan Police figure was "115 prostitutes on the street at any one time in London". "Adding these and scaling up by UK population ... gives c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country." For C, "Following a Netherlands assumption with some research support", ONS calculated estimates at 20, 25 and 30 clients a week.

The price, £55 in 2004, is "an estimate from Punternet supported by a 2004 journal article". Footnote 12, for Punternet, is a Wikipedia link. For other years: "As there is no time series, we vary the point estimates in time using the male population". The series ONS publishes, MNC8, begins in 1985.

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS published the assumptions and its own assessment of them. Assumption 1 is that "the number of prostitutes has the same pattern through time as the 16+ male population. This is a weak assumption".

Table 3, on drugs: "We assume that the purity-adjusted amount of drugs consumed by the average user remains constant over time. This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." "We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption." "The government final consumption expenditure assumption is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs." The article uses "probably false" 3 times.

In October 2014 Peter Patterson, ONS's Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote in Civil Service Quarterly that "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!)" found spending on drugs and prostitution "contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP".

## The bit continues

In 2016 ONS reduced the prostitution estimates "by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes. Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

An ONS freedom of information reply gives prostitution spending in 2010 as £5.6bn. MNC8 now gives the same year as £4,370m. By this site's arithmetic, not ONS's: £4,370m is 78% of £5.6bn, and 40 of 52 weeks is 77%.

In 2020 the series fell from £5,993m to £4,130m. By 2024 it was £6,431m.
