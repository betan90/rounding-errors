## The incident

On 29 May 2014 the Office for National Statistics (ONS) published "Changes to National Accounts: Inclusion of Illegal Drugs and Prostitution in the UK National Accounts". The rule came from the European System of National Accounts 1995 (ESA95), whose section 1.13 "states that illegal transactions to which all units involved consent are included within the production boundary". Gross national income, the article notes, "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget", and a 2012 audit had identified areas for improvement "which Member States have to address by 2014."

Prostitution's annual volume was "P*C*52". The charity Eaves "estimated that there were approx 7000 off-street prostitutes in London" in 2004, by "calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". The Metropolitan Police figure was "115 prostitutes on the street at any one time in London". Adding them and scaling up by UK population "gives c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country." Clients per week followed "a Netherlands assumption with some research support": 20, 25 and 30. The price, £55 in 2004, was "an estimate from Punternet supported by a 2004 journal article". ONS's footnote for Punternet is its Wikipedia page.

To move the count through time, ONS assumed "the number of prostitutes has the same pattern through time as the 16+ male population. This is a weak assumption ... It is necessary because we have no time series data for the number of prostitutes." Another assumption: "all prostitutes and pimps are self-employed. This is a controversial assumption".

From the drugs table: "We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption." Consumption per user, purity-adjusted, held constant: "This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it."

"The government final consumption expenditure assumption is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS published the method with its own grades attached: weak, controversial, arbitrary, probably false. "Probably false" appears 3 times in the article.

The article "does not include numerical impacts". In October 2014 Peter Patterson, ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote on the Civil Service Quarterly blog that "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!)" put drugs and prostitution at "up to £10 billion a year" of GDP.

A November 2015 ONS freedom of information reply gave drugs and illegal prostitution as £9,097m in 2010, 0.58% of GDP.

## The bit continues

In Blue Book 2016, ONS reduced the prostitution estimates "by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes. Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

The 2015 reply gave prostitution in 2010 as £5.6bn. Series MNC8, released on 31 October 2025, gives £4,370m. By this site's arithmetic, not ONS's, £4,370m is 0.78 of £5.6bn, and 40 weeks is 0.77 of 52.
