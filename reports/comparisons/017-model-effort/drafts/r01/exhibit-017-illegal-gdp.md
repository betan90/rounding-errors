## The incident

In May 2014 the Office for National Statistics published how illegal drugs and prostitution would enter UK GDP. The reason, per the article by Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew: gross national income "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget".

The rule was older: ESA95 already included consensual illegal transactions. ONS's footnote: "There are no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA10."

The prostitution estimate starts in London in 2004. The charity Eaves "estimated that there were approx 7000 off-street prostitutes in London in that year; this was done by calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". The Metropolitan Police supplied "115 prostitutes on the street at any one time in London". Scaled up by UK population, that "gives c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country."

Clients per week followed "a Netherlands assumption with some research support": estimates based on 20, 25 and 30. The price, £55 in 2004, was "an estimate from Punternet supported by a 2004 journal article". Footnote 12, for Punternet, is a Wikipedia URL.

"As there is no time series, we vary the point estimates in time using the male population". ONS called this "a weak assumption".

## Who lied

Nobody. Every assumption was published, and several were graded by the people who made them.

On drugs: "We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption." On production: "We assume that no drugs other than cannabis are produced in the UK. While this is probably false, we do not have any evidence to the contrary." "Probably false" appears 3 times in the article.

1 assumption was rated reasonable: "The government final consumption expenditure assumption is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

The article "does not include numerical impacts". In an October 2014 Civil Service Quarterly blog, Peter Patterson, ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote that "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!)" put the contribution at "up to £10 billion a year".

## The bit continues

ONS's Blue Book 2016 revisions article reduced the prostitution estimate "by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes. Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

An ONS Freedom of Information reply gave prostitution in 2010 as "£5.6bn (current prices), which is 0.4% of GDP." Today's series, MNC8, gives 2010 as £4,370m. By this site's arithmetic, the ratio is 0.78, and 40 weeks out of 52 is 0.77. ONS does not say the holiday is the whole difference.

MNC8 starts in 1985, 29 years before the first estimate was published. It shows £5,993m in 2019, £4,130m in 2020 and £6,431m in 2024. The latest release was 31 October 2025.
