## The incident

In May 2014 the Office for National Statistics (ONS) published how it would add illegal drugs and prostitution to the UK national accounts. The rule was not new: the European System of National Accounts 1995 counts "illegal transactions to which all units involved consent" within the production boundary. The timing came from an audit. Gross national income "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget", and in 2012 "a number of areas for improvement were identified which Member States have to address by 2014."

The charity Eaves estimated about 7,000 off-street prostitutes in London in 2004, "by calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". The Metropolitan Police gave "115 prostitutes on the street at any one time in London". Adding these and scaling up by UK population "gives c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country." Clients per week follow "a Netherlands assumption with some research support": 20, 25 and 30. The price, £55 in 2004, is "an estimate from Punternet supported by a 2004 journal article". The footnote for Punternet links to Wikipedia.

Over time, the number moves with the 16+ male population. ONS: "This is a weak assumption ... It is necessary because we have no time series data for the number of prostitutes."

For drugs: "We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption." The assumption on government spending "is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS published every assumption and labelled several itself. The phrase "probably false" appears 3 times in the article, and "arbitrary" twice. On drug purity: "This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it."

Peter Patterson, ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote on the Civil Service Quarterly blog in October 2014: "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!) into the numbers of prostitutes and the price of drugs yielded the result that spending on these illegal activities in the UK contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP."

## The bit continues

In Blue Book 2016, ONS reduced the prostitution estimates "by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes. Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

An ONS freedom of information reply gives prostitution in 2010 as £5.6bn. Series MNC8, last released 31 October 2025, gives £4,370m. This site's arithmetic, not ONS's: £4,370m is 78% of £5.6bn, and 40 weeks are 77% of 52. None of the ONS sources here says the holiday is the whole difference.

MNC8 begins in 1985, 29 years before ONS first published an estimate. In 2020 it fell 31%, from £5,993m to £4,130m. In 2024 it stood at £6,431m.
