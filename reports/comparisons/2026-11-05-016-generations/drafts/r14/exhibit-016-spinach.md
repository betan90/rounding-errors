## The incident

In December 1981 the BMJ's Christmas issue ran "Fake!" by T.J. Hamblin, a survey of "Frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes". A single paragraph covered spinach. Its reputation as a source of iron, Hamblin wrote, dated from the 1890s, and "the propaganda was fraudulent; German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value." His conclusion: "For a source of iron Popeye would have been better off chewing the cans."

The paragraph has no citation. The article lists 13 publications it was "derived from". None is about spinach.

## Who lied

Nobody, on the record. Hamblin was not the first. The earliest version Rekdal (2014) found is from Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, University of London: "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point." Kronick in 1985 was also hedged: "It has been suggested." Hamblin dropped the hedge and gave no source.

Sutton, a Reader in Criminology at Nottingham Trent University, emailed Hamblin. Sutton reports that Hamblin "honestly cannot remember", that the BMJ's editor had asked him for a humorous Christmas piece and asked him "not to provide references", and that Hamblin was "sure that he had not made it up."

The tellings disagree on the date. Rekdal: sources "mutually confirmed the 'fact' that a decimal point error was made, whether it was in 1870, in the 1890s, or in the 1930s." Sutton quotes a 2009 Royal Society of Chemistry book offering 2 versions: a decimal point misplaced about 20 years before 1890, or von Bunge reporting 35 mg per 100 g in 1890 from dried spinach. Sutton cites dried-spinach measurements of 44.8 mg per 100 g (1938) and about 34 (2009). The USDA lists raw spinach at 2.71. On the second version, the high figure was a correct measurement of dried spinach.

In 1995 Larsson published "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation" in the Journal of Internal Medicine. It repeats the spinach story, citing Hamblin. Rekdal: "Larsson obviously has an important message, but he manages to do exactly what he is criticizing others for in quite a spectacular way."

## The fix

Sutton went looking. Among his questions: "Who is von Wolff and where are his erroneous findings recorded?" and who the 1930s chemists were. Weeks of searching produced no answers.

He also read the Thimble Theatre strips from 1928 to 1935. In the strip of 3 July 1932, the first in which Popeye explains why he eats spinach, the reason is vitamin A. Of "hundreds of Popeye cartoons studied", Sutton reports, none links spinach to iron.

Rekdal opened his 2014 paper on academic urban legends by citing Larsson, then disclosed his method: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson." It was a demonstration.
