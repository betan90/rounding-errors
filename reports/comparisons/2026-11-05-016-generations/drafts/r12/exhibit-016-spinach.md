## The incident

In the BMJ's Christmas issue of 19 December 1981, T.J. Hamblin published a piece called "Fake!", on "frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes". One was spinach. "The discovery that spinach was as valuable a source of iron as red meat was made in the 1890s," he wrote. German chemists in the 1930s "had shown that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value." His conclusion: "For a source of iron Popeye would have been better off chewing the cans."

The paragraph carried no citation. The article ended with 13 references, none about spinach. Sutton later counted the same 13 and found that none "cite the source of the decimal error or 1930's Germans".

## Who lied

Nobody says, and no one has produced the error. Hamblin stated it as fact without a source. He did not originate it. In a 1972 inaugural lecture at the University of London, the nutritionist Bender had already hedged that "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point". Kronick repeated the hedge in 1985.

Sutton, a criminologist at Nottingham Trent University, emailed Hamblin in 2010. Sutton reports that Hamblin "wrote that he has been asked for this before and that he honestly cannot remember", that the BMJ editor had asked for a humorous piece and "asked not to provide references", and that Hamblin "was sure that he had not made it up".

Sutton asked who made the original measurement and who the 1930s chemists were. Weeks of searching answered neither. A Royal Society of Chemistry textbook offered 2 rival versions: von Bunge found 35 mg per 100 g in 1890 but had measured dried spinach, or von Wolf misplaced a decimal around 1870. "Wherever lies the truth, the error was not picked up until 1937."

Reading the Popeye strips from 1928 to 1935, Sutton reports that the character, when he first explained why he ate spinach, on 3 July 1932, credited vitamin A.

## The bit continues

In 1995 Larsson repeated the story, citing Hamblin, in a paper titled "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation".

In 2014 Rekdal catalogued the tellings, which placed the error in 1870, the 1890s, or the 1930s. He had lifted his own Hamblin citation from Larsson, on purpose, to make the point. His verdict: "By using the non-existent, or at least undocumented, decimal point error as an illustration of the importance of accuracy and critical investigation, Bender, Hamblin, Larsson, and Skrabanek and McCormick all end up doing the opposite of what they are preaching."
