## The incident

For decades, nutrition writers have repeated a specific story: spinach was once hailed as rich in iron because a researcher misplaced a decimal point and reported 10 times the true amount. The earliest version found is hedged. In a 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, Bender wrote that the vegetable's fame "may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point."

Hamblin's 1981 BMJ Christmas piece, "Fake!", dropped the hedge. German chemists, he wrote, had reinvestigated in the 1930s and found "the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate." Spinach, he concluded, was no better for you than cabbage, Brussels sprouts, or broccoli; Popeye would have done better chewing the can.

Later tellers cannot agree even on the year. Rekdal counts versions dated 1870, the 1890s, and the 1930s, independently confirming a fact none of them had traced to a document.

## Who lied

Nobody, on the documented record. Bender hedged. Hamblin stated the claim as fact and attached no citation; his article closes with a list of 13 references, and Sutton, checking in 2010, found that none of them "cite the source of the decimal error or 1930's Germans." Bender's 1972 lecture predates Hamblin's, so the story did not start with him.

Sutton emailed Hamblin directly. Sutton reports that Hamblin wrote back that he had been asked to write something humorous for the BMJ's Christmas issue and told not to supply references, that he could no longer remember his source, and that he was "sure that he had not made it up."

Larsson repeated the story in 1995, in a paper titled "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation," citing Hamblin. Sutton went looking for the underlying measurement anyway: who was von Wolff, where were his findings, who were the 1930s chemists. Weeks of searching answered none of it. He found two candidate origins instead, via Coultate's 2009 account: either von Bunge measured 35mg per 100g in 1890 but had analysed dried spinach, or von Wolf misplaced a decimal some 20 years earlier. Dried spinach measured later came in at 44.8mg per 100g (Jackson, 1938) and about 34mg per 100g (Oladele and Obarisaide, 2009); raw spinach today is listed at 2.71mg per 100g (USDA). The tenfold gap the story blames on a typo is roughly the gap between dried and fresh.

Sutton also read the Popeye newspaper strips, 1928 to 1935. None of them tie spinach to iron. Creator Segar's own strip, 3 July 1932, credits the vegetable's vitamin A instead.

## The bit continues

Rekdal opens his own 2014 paper by citing Larsson for the Hamblin reference, then tells readers: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson." His verdict covers the whole chain: Bender, Hamblin, Larsson, and Skrabanek and McCormick, all writing about the importance of accurate citation, "end up doing the opposite of what they are preaching." No correction to the BMJ piece has surfaced. The story is still told as the standard case of an error nobody caught.
