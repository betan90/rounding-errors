## The incident

"The discovery that spinach was as valuable a source of iron as red meat was made in the 1890s," T.J. Hamblin wrote in the BMJ in 1981. German chemists reinvestigating in the 1930s "had shown that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value." For iron, "Popeye would have been better off chewing the cans." The piece ran in the Christmas issue, under the heading "Frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes." It lists 13 references. None is about spinach.

## Who lied

Nobody, on the record. The earliest version Rekdal traced is hedged: Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College said "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point." Hamblin stated it as fact and gave no source for it.

Sutton, a criminologist at Nottingham Trent University, emailed Hamblin in 2010. Sutton reports that Hamblin wrote he had been asked for this before and "honestly cannot remember," that the BMJ had asked for a humorous Christmas piece and "not to provide references," and that "he was sure that he had not made it up."

In 1995 Larsson repeated the story in the Journal of Internal Medicine, citing Hamblin. His paper was titled "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation." Rekdal: "Larsson obviously has an important message, but he manages to do exactly what he is criticizing others for in quite a spectacular way."

## The bit continues

Sutton spent weeks on 4 questions, among them "Who is von Wolff and where are his erroneous findings recorded?" He answered none. No original measurement, and no 1930s German reanalysis, has been traced to a primary document.

What survives is a definitions problem. Sutton quotes a 2009 Royal Society of Chemistry text giving 2 rival origins: von Bunge's 1890 figure of 35 mg per 100 g, measured on dried spinach, or an earlier misplaced decimal. Dried-spinach measurements of 44.8 mg (1938) and about 34 mg (2009) are real. A correct figure for dried spinach, read as fresh, looks like a tenfold error. USDA lists raw spinach at 2.71 mg per 100 g.

Reading the strips for 1928 to 1935, Sutton reports that the first time Popeye explains why he eats spinach, on 3 July 1932, he credits vitamin A.

Rekdal, who opens his own paper by admitting "I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson," delivers the verdict: by using "the non-existent, or at least undocumented, decimal point error as an illustration of the importance of accuracy and critical investigation, Bender, Hamblin, Larsson, and Skrabanek and McCormick all end up doing the opposite of what they are preaching."
