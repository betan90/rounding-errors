## The incident

For decades the spinach story has been the textbook case of an error nobody corrected. In the 1890s, it goes, a decimal point went in the wrong place and made spinach look 10 times richer in iron than it is.

The most quoted telling is Hamblin's "Fake!", a BMJ Christmas piece from 19 December 1981. It says German chemists "had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value." That paragraph has no citation. The article ends with 13 publications it "derived from", none about spinach. Sutton reports that none of them "cite the source of the decimal error or 1930's Germans".

The earliest version Rekdal found is older. Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, University of London, said the fame of spinach "may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point". Tellings disagree on when the error happened. Rekdal notes "a large number of apparently independent sources" that "mutually confirmed" it, whether it was in 1870, in the 1890s, or in the 1930s.

## Who lied

The papers do not accuse anyone. Bender hedged. Kronick's 1985 version also hedges: "It has been suggested that..." Hamblin stated the story as fact.

Sutton emailed Hamblin. Sutton reports that Hamblin "wrote that he has been asked for this before and that he honestly cannot remember," and that in 1981 the BMJ's editor had asked him for a humorous Christmas piece and "asked not to provide references." Hamblin closed by assuring Sutton that he was "sure that he had not made it up."

Sutton's search for the source found nothing. He asked "Who is von Wolff and where are his erroneous findings recorded?" and, after weeks of searching, could not answer. He also read the Thimble Theatre strips from 1928 to 1935. Of hundreds of Popeye cartoons, he reports, none links spinach to iron. The strip of 3 July 1932, where Popeye first explains why he eats spinach, credits vitamin A. "In fact, Segar chose and promoted spinach for its vitamin A content alone," Sutton concludes.

Larsson's 1995 paper, "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation", repeats the story and cites Hamblin.

## The bit continues

Rekdal's open-access paper opens by citing Larsson, then admits: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson." His conclusion on the whole chain: "By using the non-existent, or at least undocumented, decimal point error as an illustration of the importance of accuracy and critical investigation, Bender, Hamblin, Larsson, and Skrabanek and McCormick all end up doing the opposite of what they are preaching."
