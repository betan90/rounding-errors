## The incident

Hamblin dates the discovery to the 1890s: spinach was as valuable a source of iron as red meat. In his 1981 BMJ Christmas article he adds that German chemists had shown in the 1930s that the original workers "had put the decimal point in the wrong place", producing a figure 10 times too high. That paragraph has no citation. None of the article's 13 references, Sutton counts, concerns the decimal error or the 1930s chemists.

Sutton, a criminologist at Nottingham Trent University, spent weeks searching. He could not say who von Wolff was or where his erroneous findings were recorded, and could not name the 1930s chemists. Coultate (2009), as Sutton quotes him, offers two versions. Von Bunge in 1890 found 35 mg of iron per 100 g, but had analysed dried spinach. Or von Wolff, about 20 years earlier, misplaced the decimal point. "Wherever lies the truth, the error was not picked up until 1937."

Of the hundreds of Popeye cartoons Sutton studied, none links spinach with iron. In the strip of 3 July 1932, the first in which Popeye explains why he eats spinach, the credit goes to vitamin A.

## Who lied

The record accuses no one. Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture, the earliest version Rekdal found, says spinach's fame "may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point". Kronick's 1985 version is hedged too. Hamblin stated the story without a source. Sutton reports Hamblin's email: he had been asked to write a humorous piece for the Christmas edition, and asked not to provide references. He had been asked about this before and could not remember. He was sure, he wrote, "that he had not made it up."

Larsson's 1995 paper, "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation," repeats the story and cites Hamblin for it.

## The bit continues

Rekdal's 2014 paper, "Academic urban legends," opens by citing Larsson. It then confesses: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson." He did this on purpose. He notes that "a large number of apparently independent sources have mutually confirmed the 'fact' that a decimal point error was made," whether in 1870, the 1890s, or the 1930s.
