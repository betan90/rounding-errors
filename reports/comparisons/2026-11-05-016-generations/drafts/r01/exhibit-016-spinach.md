## The incident

In 1981 T.J. Hamblin wrote a Christmas article for the BMJ titled "Fake!" He described spinach as a case study: "The discovery that spinach was as valuable a source of iron as red meat was made in the 1890s. Unfortunately, the propaganda was fraudulent; German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value."

Hamblin's article listed 13 references. None of them documented the decimal point error.

The story did not originate with Hamblin. In 1972, A.E. Bender, speaking at Queen Elizabeth College, University of London, had said: "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point." Bender hedged the claim.

## Who lied

Nobody. Bender hedged. Hamblin stated it as fact, unsourced. Sutton reports that when he asked Hamblin for the source, Hamblin replied he had been asked to write a humorous Christmas article without references and could not recall the origin. Hamblin assured Sutton he had not invented it.

In 1995 Lennart Larsson published "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation," warning against repeating claims without scrutinizing their sources. Larsson's paper repeated the spinach story, citing Hamblin, providing none of his own.

Sutton searched for weeks: electronic databases, biochemistry specialists, archives. He could not name the German chemists, could not locate the original measurement, could not establish when the error supposedly occurred. Different versions of the story placed it at 1870, the 1890s, or the 1930s. Øyvind Rekdal, analyzing the citation chain, found that sources "have mutually confirmed the 'fact' that a decimal point error was made, whether it was in 1870, in the 1890s, or in the 1930s."

## The bit continues

Rekdal's 2014 analysis of academic legend found the irony stark: "By using the non-existent, or at least undocumented, decimal point error as an illustration of the importance of accuracy and critical investigation, Bender, Hamblin, Larsson, and Skrabanek and McCormick all end up doing the opposite of what they are preaching."

The story remains in circulation. It serves as the textbook example of an uncorrected error and of how scientific claims propagate without scrutiny. Both descriptions are true. The example itself has no documented source.
