## The incident

In the BMJ's Christmas issue of 19 December 1981, under the heading "Frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes", T.J. Hamblin wrote that the discovery that spinach was as valuable a source of iron as red meat was made in the 1890s.

"Unfortunately, the propaganda was fraudulent; German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value. Spinach is no better for you than cabbage, Brussels sprouts, or broccoli. For a source of iron Popeye would have been better off chewing the cans."

The paragraph carries no citation. The article closes with a list of 13 publications it was derived from. None is about spinach.

## Who lied

Nobody, and the hedging is in the record. The earliest version Rekdal traced is Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, University of London: the fame of spinach "may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point". Kronick, in 1985, wrote "It has been suggested that spinach got its reputation as a dietary supplement because of a misplaced decimal point". Hamblin stated it without a source.

Sutton, a criminologist at Nottingham Trent University, emailed him. Sutton reports that Hamblin wrote he had been asked for this before and honestly could not remember, that the then editor of the BMJ had asked him for a humorous piece for the Christmas edition, and that he had been asked not to provide references. Sutton adds that Hamblin "rounded off his email with the assurance that he was sure that he had not made it up."

In 1995, in the Journal of Internal Medicine, Larsson repeated the story and cited Hamblin. The paper was titled "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation".

## The bit continues

Sutton spent weeks on 4 questions. Who is von Wolff and where are his erroneous findings recorded. Who were the 1930s chemists. He answered none of them. The nearest thing to an origin he found was a 2009 Royal Society of Chemistry textbook offering 2 incompatible versions: von Bunge in 1890 measuring 35mg per 100g of spinach that had been dried, or von Wolf misplacing a decimal point about 20 years earlier.

Dried spinach has been measured at 44.8 mg of iron per 100 g in 1938 and about 34 mg in 2009. The USDA lists raw spinach at 2.71 mg per 100 g. A dried sample read as a fresh one produces a tenfold error without anyone moving a decimal point.

Sutton also read the Thimble Theatre strips from 1928 to 1935. In the strip of 3 July 1932, the first in which Popeye explains why he eats spinach, he credits vitamin A. Of the hundreds of Popeye cartoons Sutton studied, none associates spinach with iron.
