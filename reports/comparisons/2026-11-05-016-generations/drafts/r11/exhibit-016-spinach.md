## The incident

In the BMJ's 1981 Christmas issue, in an article on "Frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes", T.J. Hamblin wrote that spinach was found in the 1890s to be "as valuable a source of iron as red meat." Then: "Unfortunately, the propaganda was fraudulent; German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value." Popeye, he added, "would have been better off chewing the cans."

The paragraph carries no citation. The article lists 13 publications it was "derived from". None is about spinach.

## Who lied

Nobody, on the record. The story is older than Hamblin's article. Rekdal, writing in Social Studies of Science in 2014, found the earliest version in Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, London: "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point." In 1985 Kronick wrote: "It has been suggested..." Hamblin's article stated it as fact.

In 2010 Sutton, a criminologist at Nottingham Trent University, went looking. 1 of his questions: "Who is von Wolff and where are his erroneous findings recorded?" After weeks of searching he could not answer it. He emailed Hamblin. Sutton reports that Hamblin "honestly cannot remember," that the BMJ's then editor had asked him for a humorous Christmas piece and asked him "not to provide references," and that he was "sure that he had not made it up."

Sutton also quotes a Royal Society of Chemistry book (Coultate, 2009) offering 2 versions: a misplaced decimal point, or von Bunge's 1890 figure of 35 mg per 100 g, measured on dried spinach. Sutton cites dried spinach at 44.8 mg per 100 g in 1938 and about 34 in 2009. USDA lists raw spinach at 2.71. The high figure may be a correct measurement of a different thing.

The tellings also disagree on the date. Rekdal: sources have confirmed the error "whether it was in 1870, in the 1890s, or in the 1930s."

Sutton read the Thimble Theatre strips from 1928 to 1935. In the strip of 3 July 1932, the first in which Popeye explains why he eats spinach, the reason is vitamin A. Sutton reports that none of the "hundreds of Popeye cartoons studied" links spinach to iron.

## The bit continues

In 1995 the Journal of Internal Medicine published Larsson's "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation". It repeats the spinach story, citing Hamblin. Rekdal: "Larsson obviously has an important message, but he manages to do exactly what he is criticizing others for in quite a spectacular way."

Rekdal opens his own paper by citing Larsson, on purpose, then explains: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson."
