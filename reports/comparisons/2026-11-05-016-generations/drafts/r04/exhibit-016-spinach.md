## The incident

The story: A decimal point error made spinach's reputation for iron. Someone measuring spinach in the 1890s (or 1870s, or 1930s, depending on who tells it) placed a decimal incorrectly, making the iron content 10 times higher than it actually was. German chemists corrected it, and the error became a textbook example of how sloppy science travels unchecked.

Hamblin wrote in the BMJ in 1981: "The discovery that spinach was as valuable a source of iron as red meat was made in the 1890s. Unfortunately, the propaganda was fraudulent; German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value."

## Who lied

Nobody. Bender introduced it in 1972, hedged: the fame of spinach "may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point." Hamblin restated it in 1981 as settled fact, but offered no source. The article containing the claim had 13 references; none addressed the decimal error.

"Hamblin wrote that he has been asked for this before and that he honestly cannot remember," Sutton reports. "He said that back in 1981 he had been asked to write a humorous piece by the then editor of the British Medical Journal for its Christmas edition and that he had been asked not to provide references."

Sutton, a criminology reader at Nottingham Trent, spent weeks in 2010 searching for the original. He could not answer basic questions: "Who is von Wolff and where are his erroneous findings recorded?" He found no chemist names, no primary documents, no proof the error ever happened as described.

Larsson repeated the story in 1995 in an article titled "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation." Rekdal notes: "Larsson obviously has an important message, but he manages to do exactly what he is criticizing others for in quite a spectacular way." Rekdal himself opens his own paper by citing Larsson's Hamblin reference, then pauses: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson." He does this deliberately, to show how readily a doubtful claim travels.

## The bit continues

The story endures. It is cited as a cautionary tale: evidence of how citation errors compound when no one checks the original source. Yet the original source has never been produced. Bender hedged in 1972. Hamblin stated it without evidence in 1981. Larsson repeated it in 1995 while warning others against repeating unverified claims. Each time the story moved further from its supposed origin.

Rekdal's examination captures the irony: "By using the non-existent, or at least undocumented, decimal point error as an illustration of the importance of accuracy and critical investigation, Bender, Hamblin, Larsson, and Skrabanek and McCormick all end up doing the opposite of what they are preaching."
