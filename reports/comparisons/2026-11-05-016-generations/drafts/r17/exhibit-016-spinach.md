## The incident

For decades, nutrition writers have told the same story to illustrate the danger of an uncorrected error. Spinach was declared rich in iron in the late 19th century. The figure was 10 times too high. Someone misplaced a decimal point, and the mistake stood for decades before anyone caught it.

T.J. Hamblin told it in the BMJ's 1981 Christmas issue, in an article about scientific fraud: "German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value." Popeye, he added, would have done better chewing the can.

The article lists 13 references it was "derived from." None concerns spinach, iron, or a decimal point.

## Who lied

Nobody, on the documented record, though the hedging thinned with each retelling. The earliest traced version is Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College: "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point." Hedged, and 9 years ahead of Hamblin. Kronick's 1985 version kept the hedge: "It has been suggested that..."

Hamblin, in 1981, dropped it and stated the error as fact, with no citation. Asked about it decades later, Hamblin emailed the criminologist Mike Sutton. Sutton reports that Hamblin wrote he had been asked for this before and "honestly cannot remember," that the BMJ's editor had commissioned a humorous Christmas piece and told him not to provide references, and that he "was sure that he had not made it up."

Sutton went looking for the original measurement and the 1930s German reanalysis. He asked, in print, who the chemists were and where their findings were recorded. Weeks of searching found nothing. One account he cites offers 2 competing stories: a measurement of 35mg of iron per 100g in 1890, but of dried spinach, not fresh; or an earlier figure with a misplaced decimal. Later dried-spinach measurements run close to the supposedly erroneous number, so the "error" may be dried spinach mistaken for fresh.

Larsson, in a 1995 paper titled "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation," repeated the story, citing Hamblin. Rekdal, writing in 2014 about academic urban legends, opened his own paper by citing Larsson, then disclosed: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson."

## The bit continues

Sutton also read the Popeye strips, 1928 to 1935. None mentions iron. The strip of 3 July 1932, the first where Popeye explains himself, credits vitamin A.

The textbook example of an uncorrected error remains uncorrected, because there was never a traced correction behind it, only a story about one, told and retold by people warning against exactly this.
