## The incident

The story: a decimal point slipped, and spinach was credited with 10 times the iron it has. It is told as the standard example of a scientific error nobody corrected.

The earliest version Rekdal found is Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, University of London: "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point". Kronick, in 1985, also hedged: "It has been suggested that spinach got its reputation as a dietary supplement because of a misplaced decimal point."

The BMJ Christmas issue of 1981 dropped the hedge. T.J. Hamblin wrote that German chemists "had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate". Spinach, he added, was "no better for you than cabbage, Brussels sprouts, or broccoli."

The paragraph has no citation. The article lists 13 references. Sutton counted the same 13 and found that none of them cites the source of the decimal error or the 1930s Germans.

## Who lied

No institution has said anyone did. The article's own framing was "Frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes".

Mike Sutton, a criminologist at Nottingham Trent University, asked who von Wolff was, where his erroneous findings were recorded, and who the 1930s chemists were. After weeks of searching he could not answer. He emailed Hamblin. Sutton reports that Hamblin replied that he had been asked before and could not remember, that the BMJ editor had asked for a humorous piece without references, and that he was sure he had not made it up.

Bender's 1972 lecture precedes Hamblin, so the trail does not start in 1981. The dates do not agree either. Rekdal: "a large number of apparently independent sources have mutually confirmed the 'fact' that a decimal point error was made, whether it was in 1870, in the 1890s, or in the 1930s."

Sutton cites Coultate's 2009 book for 2 competing versions. In one, von Bunge found 35 mg of iron per 100 g in 1890 but had analysed dried spinach. Sutton cites dried-spinach measurements of 44.8 mg per 100 g (Jackson, 1938). USDA lists raw spinach at 2.71 mg.

## The bit continues

In 1995 Larsson published "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation" in the Journal of Internal Medicine. It repeats the spinach story and cites Hamblin. Rekdal: "Larsson obviously has an important message, but he manages to do exactly what he is criticizing others for in quite a spectacular way."

Rekdal opens his own paper by citing Larsson, then states: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson." He meant it as a demonstration.

Sutton also read the Thimble Theatre strips from 1928 to 1935. He reports that the strip of 3 July 1932, the first in which Popeye explains why he eats spinach, credits vitamin A, and that in hundreds of strips none links spinach to iron.
