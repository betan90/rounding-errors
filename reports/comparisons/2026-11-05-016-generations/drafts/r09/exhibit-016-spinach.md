## The incident

In 1981 the BMJ's Christmas issue published a piece on "Frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes." Its author, T.J. Hamblin, offered a parable: "The discovery that spinach was as valuable a source of iron as red meat was made in the 1890s... German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value."

The passage has no citation. The article lists 13 publications it "derived from"; none concerns spinach.

## Who lied

Nobody is accused. The earliest version Rekdal found, in Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, hedges: "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point." Hamblin's 1981 version states it as fact.

Sutton emailed Hamblin. Sutton reports that Hamblin wrote he had been asked for this before and honestly could not remember. He had been asked to write a humorous piece for the BMJ's Christmas edition and not to provide references. He was sure, Sutton reports, that he had not made it up.

The dates disagree across tellings. Rekdal: "a large number of apparently independent sources have mutually confirmed the 'fact' that a decimal point error was made, whether it was in 1870, in the 1890s, or in the 1930s."

Sutton searched for weeks. Among his questions: "Who is von Wolff and where are his erroneous findings recorded?" He could not answer them. The measurement-definitions twist: the high figure may have been a correct measurement of dried spinach, read as fresh.

## The bit continues

Larsson's 1995 paper in the Journal of Internal Medicine is titled "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation." It repeats the decimal point story, citing Hamblin. Rekdal: "Larsson obviously has an important message, but he manages to do exactly what he is criticizing others for in quite a spectacular way."

Rekdal's own 2014 paper opens by citing Larsson, then admits: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson." It is a deliberate demonstration. His finding: the textbook example of an uncorrected error, cited by authors warning about uncorrected errors, has no documented source.

Sutton found another angle. He read the bound volumes of the Thimble Theatre strips from 1928 to 1935. On 3 July 1932, the first strip where Popeye explains why he eats spinach, he credits vitamin A. Of hundreds of cartoons studied, Sutton reports, none associates spinach with iron.
