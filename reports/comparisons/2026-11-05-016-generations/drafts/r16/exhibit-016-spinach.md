## The incident

The story appears in dozens of papers. A 19th-century chemist measured the iron in spinach and put the decimal point in the wrong place, making it look 10 times richer than it was. Decades later, German scientists corrected the error, but by then spinach had its reputation. In some versions Popeye was involved.

The story is usually told to illustrate the danger of uncorrected scientific mistakes. It is also uncorrected, and it has no source.

T.J. Hamblin wrote it in the BMJ in 1981. His article, titled "Fake!" and published in the Christmas issue, listed 13 references. None supported the spinach claim. The passage: "The discovery that spinach was as valuable a source of iron as red meat was made in the 1890s... Unfortunately, the propaganda was fraudulent; German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value."

Mike Sutton, a criminologist at Nottingham Trent, searched for the original measurement. He read papers, databases, and the original Popeye strips from 1928 to 1935. He emailed Hamblin in 2010. Sutton reports that Hamblin said he could not remember where he got the story and was sure he had not made it up. Hamblin also said the BMJ editor had asked him not to provide references.

## Who lied

Nobody, on the record. The record contains people stating the story, hedging about it, and failing to find its source.

The earliest version Ole Bjørn Rekdal traced was Arnold Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, University of London. Bender hedged: the fame of spinach "may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point." Hamblin stated it as fact in 1981, with no citation. In 1995 Sture Larsson repeated it in the Journal of Internal Medicine, citing Hamblin. The paper's title: "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation."

Rekdal, writing in 2014 about academic urban legends, admitted he copied the Hamblin reference from Larsson without checking it, as a deliberate demonstration. His line: "By using the non-existent, or at least undocumented, decimal point error as an illustration of the importance of accuracy and critical investigation, Bender, Hamblin, Larsson, and Skrabanek and McCormick all end up doing the opposite of what they are preaching."

The year of the supposed error varies by telling. Rekdal catalogued versions placing it in 1870, the 1890s, or the 1930s.

Sutton found a competing explanation. The high figure may be a real measurement of dried spinach, which concentrates iron, later read as fresh. The USDA lists raw spinach at 2.71 milligrams of iron per 100 grams.

Sutton also checked Popeye. The first strip where Popeye explains why he eats spinach, 3 July 1932, credits vitamin A. Of hundreds of cartoons Sutton studied, none mentioned iron.

## The bit continues

The story is still told. Rekdal's paper, citing it as an example of uncorrected error, was published in 2014. The original 19th-century measurement it describes has never been produced.
