## The incident

The story runs like this: sometime in the late 19th or early 20th century, chemists measured the iron content of spinach. A decimal point went in the wrong place. The published figure was 10 times too high. Spinach became famous. Popeye ate it. Decades later, other chemists caught the error.

T.J. Hamblin told the story in the BMJ's 1981 Christmas issue, in an article on frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularized mistakes. "The discovery that spinach was as valuable a source of iron as red meat was made in the 1890s," he wrote. "Unfortunately, the propaganda was fraudulent; German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value."

The article lists 13 publications it derived from. None is about spinach.

## Who lied

Nobody, on the available record. The earliest version Ole Bjørn Rekdal found, in Arnold Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, London, was hedged: the fame of spinach "may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point." Hamblin stated it as fact 9 years later, with no citation.

Mike Sutton, a criminology researcher at Nottingham Trent, read Hamblin and went looking. He emailed Hamblin in 2010. Sutton reports that Hamblin wrote back saying he honestly could not remember the source, that he had been asked to write a humorous piece for the BMJ Christmas edition and asked not to provide references, and that he was sure he had not made it up.

Sutton spent weeks searching. His 4 questions included: "Who is von Wolff and where are his erroneous findings recorded?" and "Who were the 1930s chemists who discovered the decimal place error and where are their findings recorded?" He could not answer them.

One hypothesis: the high figure may be a real measurement of dried spinach, interpreted as fresh. Dried spinach measured in the 1930s ran 34 to 45 mg of iron per 100 g. Fresh spinach today measures 2.71 mg per 100 g.

Sutton also read the bound volumes of the Thimble Theatre comic strips from 1928 to 1935. The strip of 3 July 1932, the first where Popeye explains why he eats spinach, credits vitamin A. Of hundreds of Popeye cartoons Sutton studied, none associated spinach with iron.

## The bit continues

Rekdal, writing in 2014 about academic urban legends, catalogued versions of the story. The dates disagree: the error was made in 1870, or the 1890s, or the 1930s, depending on who tells it. In 1995 Sune Larsson published a paper titled "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation." It repeats the decimal point story, citing Hamblin.

Rekdal's verdict: "By using the non-existent, or at least undocumented, decimal point error as an illustration of the importance of accuracy and critical investigation, Bender, Hamblin, Larsson, and Skrabanek and McCormick all end up doing the opposite of what they are preaching."

The textbook example of an uncorrected error has no traceable source. It was retold by people warning about uncorrected errors.
