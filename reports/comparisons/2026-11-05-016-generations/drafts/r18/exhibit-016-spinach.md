## The incident

In the BMJ Christmas issue of 19 December 1981, T.J. Hamblin published "Fake!", an article on "frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes". It included spinach.

"German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value. Spinach is no better for you than cabbage, Brussels sprouts, or broccoli. For a source of iron Popeye would have been better off chewing the cans."

The paragraph has no citation. The article ends with 13 sources. Sutton, a Reader in Criminology at Nottingham Trent University, counted them in 2010. None cites the decimal error or the 1930s Germans.

## Who lied

The record holds a hedge, a statement and a repetition.

The hedge is Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, London: the fame of spinach "may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point". It is the earliest version Rekdal found. The statement is Hamblin's, 9 years later, without the "may". The repetition is Larsson's, in 1995, in the Journal of Internal Medicine, in a paper titled "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation". Larsson cited Hamblin. Rekdal: Larsson "manages to do exactly what he is criticizing others for in quite a spectacular way."

Sutton emailed Hamblin. Sutton reports that Hamblin wrote that he had been asked before and "honestly cannot remember". The BMJ's editor had asked for a humorous piece and asked him not to provide references. Sutton also reports that Hamblin was sure he had not made it up.

Rekdal then opens his own paper with Larsson's citation of Hamblin and admits: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson." It is a demonstration.

## The bit continues

The tellings disagree on the year. Rekdal notes "a large number of apparently independent sources have mutually confirmed" the error, "whether it was in 1870, in the 1890s, or in the 1930s".

Sutton asked who von Wolff was and who the 1930s chemists were. After weeks of searching he could not say. He quotes Coultate (2009) with 2 versions: von Bunge found 35 mg per 100 g in 1890 but had analysed dried spinach, or von Wolf misplaced the decimal point some 20 years earlier. Dried spinach measures 44.8 mg per 100 g (Jackson, 1938). The high figure may have been a correct measurement of the wrong food. USDA lists raw spinach at 2.71 mg.

Sutton also read the Thimble Theatre strips of 1928 to 1935. He reports that the strip of 3 July 1932, the first to explain why Popeye eats spinach, credits vitamin A, and that none of the hundreds he studied mentions iron.
