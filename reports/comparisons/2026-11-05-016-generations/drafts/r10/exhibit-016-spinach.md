## The incident

The story runs like this: in the 1890s (or 1870, or 1930s, depending on who tells it), a German scientist measured the iron content of spinach. Someone put the decimal point in the wrong place, making spinach appear 10 times richer in iron than it is. The error went uncorrected for decades and spawned Popeye.

T.J. Hamblin told this version in the BMJ's 1981 Christmas issue: "German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value."

The article's subject was scientific fraud: "Frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes." It listed 13 references. None concerned spinach.

## Who lied

The earliest known version is D.A. Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, London: "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point." Bender hedged. In 1985 David Kronick hedged too: "It has been suggested that spinach got its reputation as a dietary supplement because of a misplaced decimal point."

Hamblin did not hedge. He stated it as fact, in a Christmas piece the editor had asked him to write without references. In 2010 the criminologist Mike Sutton emailed Hamblin asking for the source. Sutton reports that Hamblin "wrote that he has been asked for this before and that he honestly cannot remember." Hamblin added that "he was sure that he had not made it up."

Sutton spent weeks searching. He could not answer his own 4 questions, including: "Who is von Wolff and where are his erroneous findings recorded?" and "Who were the 1930s chemists who discovered the decimal place error?"

One complication: dried spinach is iron-dense. Sutton cites measurements of 44.8 and about 34 mg per 100 g. Raw spinach is 2.71 mg per 100 g. A 19th-century figure for dried spinach, read as fresh, would look like a tenfold error. The original measurement, in either form, has never been traced.

In 1995 Jan Larsson published "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation" in the Journal of Internal Medicine. He used the spinach story, citing Hamblin. Ole Bjørn Rekdal, in a 2014 paper on academic urban legends, writes: "Larsson obviously has an important message, but he manages to do exactly what he is criticizing others for in quite a spectacular way."

Sutton, reading the original Popeye strips, found the 3 July 1932 cartoon in which Popeye first explains why he eats spinach. His answer: vitamin A. Of hundreds of strips Sutton studied, none associated spinach with iron.

## The bit continues

Rekdal's own paper opens by citing Larsson, then admits: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson." A deliberate demonstration.

The dates across the tellings disagree: 1870, the 1890s, or the 1930s. Rekdal: "a large number of apparently independent sources have mutually confirmed the 'fact' that a decimal point error was made." The sources mutually confirm a fact. The fact has no source.
