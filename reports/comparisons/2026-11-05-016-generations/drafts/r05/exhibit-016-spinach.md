## The incident

In the BMJ's Christmas issue of 19 December 1981, the haematologist T.J. Hamblin published "Fake!", a survey of "Frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes". One paragraph has outlived the rest of it.

"The discovery that spinach was as valuable a source of iron as red meat was made in the 1890s ... Unfortunately, the propaganda was fraudulent; German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value." Popeye, Hamblin added, "would have been better off chewing the cans".

The paragraph carries no citation. The article closes with the 13 publications it was derived from. None of them is about spinach.

## Who lied

The earliest telling Rekdal found hedges. Arnold Bender's 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, University of London: "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point". Hamblin stated it, and gave no source.

In 1995 the Journal of Internal Medicine published Larsson's "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation". It repeats the spinach story and cites Hamblin. Rekdal: "Larsson obviously has an important message, but he manages to do exactly what he is criticizing others for in quite a spectacular way."

Mike Sutton, a criminologist at Nottingham Trent University, emailed Hamblin in 2010. Sutton reports that Hamblin "wrote that he has been asked for this before and that he honestly cannot remember", that the BMJ's editor had commissioned a humorous piece for the Christmas edition and asked him "not to provide references", and that he "rounded off his email with the assurance that he was sure that he had not made it up."

## The bit continues

Sutton set himself 4 questions, among them "Who is von Wolff and where are his erroneous findings recorded?" and who the 1930s chemists were. Weeks of searching answered none of them. The tellings do not agree on when the error happened: Rekdal records 1870, the 1890s, and the 1930s.

There is a real measurement underneath. Sutton quotes Coultate's 2009 Royal Society of Chemistry book, which offers 2 versions: von Bunge in 1890 found 35mg per 100g but had analysed dried spinach, or von Wolf misplaced a decimal point about 20 years earlier. Dried spinach does run that high. Sutton cites 44.8 mg per 100 g from Jackson in 1938 and about 34 mg from Oladele and Obarisaide in 2009. USDA puts raw spinach at 2.71 mg. Drying to raw is already a factor of roughly 10, with the decimal point in place.

Popeye was never the evidence. Sutton read the bound Thimble Theatre volumes from 1928 to 1935 and reports that in the first strip where Popeye explains why he eats spinach, 3 July 1932, he credits vitamin A.
