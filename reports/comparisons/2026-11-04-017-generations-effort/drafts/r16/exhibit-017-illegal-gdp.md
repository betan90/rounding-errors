## The incident

In May 2014 ONS explained how illegal drugs and prostitution would enter the UK national accounts. The rule was not new. ESA95, section 1.13, "states that illegal transactions to which all units involved consent are included within the production boundary". ONS's footnote: "There are no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA10." The occasion was a 2012 EU audit, with areas for improvement to "address by 2014".

The article, by Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew, began with "significant limitations in the availability of data". Its prostitution estimate starts in London in 2004. The charity Eaves "estimated that there were approx 7000 off-street prostitutes in London in that year; this was done by calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". The Metropolitan Police figure: "115 prostitutes on the street at any one time in London". Scaled up by UK population: "c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country."

Clients per week followed "a Netherlands assumption with some research support"; ONS calculated estimates on 20, 25 and 30. The price, £55 in 2004, was "an estimate from Punternet supported by a 2004 journal article". ONS's footnote for Punternet is http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punternet. "As there is no time series, we vary the point estimates in time using the male population." ONS's assessment: "This is a weak assumption."

The drugs estimate had its own table:

"We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption."

"We assume that the purity-adjusted amount of drugs consumed by the average user remains constant over time. This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it."

The assumption for government spending "is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS published every assumption and graded several itself: "probably false" 3 times, "arbitrary" twice, also "weak" and "controversial". The controversial one: "There is no employment in prostitution; all prostitutes and pimps are self-employed."

In October 2014 Peter Patterson, ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote on a government blog that "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!) into the numbers of prostitutes and the price of drugs" put spending on these activities at "up to £10 billion a year". The inclusion, he wrote, was "legally mandated": "Comparability between nations dictates that all countries include it in their GDP figures."

## The bit continues

An ONS FOI reply gave prostitution in 2010 as "£5.6bn (current prices), which is 0.4% of GDP".

In Blue Book 2016 ONS reduced the prostitution estimates "by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes." Statistics Netherlands had estimated 12 weeks a year, and "the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

The current series, MNC8, gives 2010 as £4,370m. ONS does not state that the holiday is the difference. This site's arithmetic: £4,370m is 78% of £5.6bn, and 40 weeks is 77% of 52.

MNC8 reads £5,993m for 2019, £4,130m for 2020 and £6,431m for 2024.
