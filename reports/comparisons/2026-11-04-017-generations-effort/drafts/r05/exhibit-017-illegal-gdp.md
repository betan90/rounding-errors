## The incident

Each EU Member State's contribution to the budget is set partly by its Gross National Income, which "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget". In 2012 an EU audit of national-accounts methods set a 2014 deadline for fixes. One was illegal activity, and the requirement was not new. The European System of National Accounts had included "illegal transactions to which all units involved consent" within the production boundary since ESA95; an ONS footnote records "There are no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA10."

In May 2014, in an article by Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew, ONS added illegal drugs and prostitution to the accounts. The method, it said, has "significant limitations in the availability of data" and "requires a number of assumptions". For the number of prostitutes it took a 2004 charity count of "approx 7000 off-street prostitutes in London", added a police count of "115 prostitutes on the street", and, "scaling up by UK population ... gives c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country". The price of a visit, £55 in 2004, came from Punternet, footnoted to en.wikipedia.org.

The drugs assumptions were stated plainly. "This is an arbitrary assumption." "The government final consumption expenditure assumption is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs." Across the article, "probably false" appears 3 times and "arbitrary" twice.

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS labelled its own assumptions. It assumed the number of prostitutes tracks the "16+ male population", "a weak assumption". It assumed "all prostitutes and pimps are self-employed", "a controversial assumption". It wrote that the estimates are "partly based" on all of it, and that the article "does not include numerical impacts".

The totals came later. On a government blog, ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser Peter Patterson reported that "ONS desk research ... into the numbers of prostitutes and the price of drugs yielded the result that spending on these illegal activities in the UK contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP".

## The bit continues

ONS still publishes the series. MNC8 runs from 1985, though the first estimate appeared in 2014. With "no time series", ONS varies a 2004 point estimate by the male population, so the line plots the assumption, not an observation. It reads £4,130m for 2020, down 31% from £5,993m in 2019. This entry found no ONS explanation for the fall.

In Blue Book 2016 ONS added "a further refinement", reducing prostitution "by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes". "Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

The 2015 FOI put 2010 prostitution at "£5.6bn". Today's series puts it at £4,370m. This site's arithmetic: £4,370m is 0.78 of £5.6bn, and 40 of 52 weeks is 0.77. ONS does not state the holiday is the whole difference.
