## The incident

In 2014 ONS began including illegal drugs and prostitution in UK GDP. The rule was not new. ESA95 "section 1.13 - states that illegal transactions to which all units involved consent are included within the production boundary", and ONS's footnote records "no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA10". A 2012 audit of EU members' methods set a 2014 deadline for improvements.

The method article, by Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew, published 29 May 2014, gives the prostitution volume as "P\*C\*52". P came from a charity's 2004 estimate of "approx 7000 off-street prostitutes in London", arrived at "by calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place", plus the Metropolitan Police's "115 prostitutes on the street at any one time in London". "Adding these and scaling up by UK population ... gives c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country." C, clients per week, follows "a Netherlands assumption with some research support": "we calculate estimates based on 20, 25 and 30." The price is "£55 in 2004", from Punternet and a 2004 journal article, moved forward with the CPI for personal services. The footnote for Punternet is a Wikipedia URL.

ONS grades its own inputs. On the number of prostitutes tracking the 16+ male population: "This is a weak assumption ... It is necessary because we have no time series data for the number of prostitutes." On employment: "There is no employment in prostitution; all prostitutes and pimps are self-employed. This is a controversial assumption". On drug users consuming a constant purity-adjusted amount: "This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." On half of UK cannabis being imported: "This is an arbitrary assumption." 1 assumption it defends: "The government final consumption expenditure assumption is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

"probably false" appears 3 times in the article. "arbitrary" appears 2 times.

## Who lied

Nobody. The assumptions are the method, published with ONS's own assessment of each one. The executive summary opens on "significant limitations in the availability of data", which "requires a number of assumptions ... This applies especially to the estimates of prostitution activity." The article "does not include numerical impacts".

Peter Patterson, then ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, on a government blog in October 2014: "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!) into the numbers of prostitutes and the price of drugs yielded the result that spending on these illegal activities in the UK contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP."

## The bit continues

Blue Book 2016 added "a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes": "Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

ONS's FOI response puts 2010 prostitution at "£5.6bn (current prices), which is 0.4% of GDP". The published series puts 2010 at £4,370m.
