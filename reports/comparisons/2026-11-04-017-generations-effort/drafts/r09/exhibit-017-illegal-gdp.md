## The incident

UK national accounts count illegal activity as production when everyone involved consents. ONS's 2014 article places the rule in ESA95 section 1.13. The reason given is GNI, which "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget."

The headcount starts from a charity's 2004 estimate of about 7,000 off-street prostitutes in London, and a Metropolitan Police count of 115 on the street at any one time. The article's count, scaled to the UK population, is "c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country." There is no time series, so "we vary the point estimates in time using the male population." The article's label: "This is a weak assumption."

The estimates run on 20, 25 and 30 clients a week, following "a Netherlands assumption with some research support". This exhibit has not found which of the 3 figures feeds the published series.

The price is "an estimate from Punternet supported by a 2004 journal article", £55 in 2004. Its footnote links to Wikipedia.

The drug table assumes half of cannabis is imported: "This is an arbitrary assumption." On government spending, the assumption is "reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

Prostitutes are treated as self-employed: "There is no employment in prostitution; all prostitutes and pimps are self-employed. This is a controversial assumption."

Patterson's blog then gave a number. ONS desk research "(please note the italics for emphasis!)" found that spending on these activities "contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP."

## Who lied

Nobody. Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew's 2014 article for ONS published its assumptions and labelled their weaknesses.

Peter Patterson, ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote on his blog that inclusion is "legally mandated through the European System of Accounts 2010". The 2014 article places the rule in ESA95 and says "There are no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA10."

## The bit continues

The series, MNC8, is still published. The release used here is dated 31 October 2025. It starts in 1985, and the first estimates were published in 2014.

2 published figures for 2010: £5.6bn in the FOI reply, at current prices, and £4,370m in the MNC8 series, also at current prices.

This site's arithmetic, not ONS's: £4,370m is 78% of £5.6bn. 40 of 52 weeks, after the 12-week holiday, is 77%. ONS does not state that the holiday is the whole difference, and this exhibit does not either.

Blue Book 2016 added the holiday as "a further refinement". Its allowance reduces the prostitution estimates "to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes". Statistics Netherlands estimated "the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year", and "the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

The series falls from £5,993m in 2019 to £4,130m in 2020, then stands at £6,142m in 2022. No ONS source this exhibit found gives a cause for the fall.
