## The incident

Gross national income "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget". In 2012, after "a comprehensive audit of the methods used across EU countries, a number of areas for improvement were identified which Member States have to address by 2014". Among them was illegal activity. The European System of National Accounts 1995, section 1.13, places illegal transactions "to which all units involved consent" inside the production boundary. ONS records no changes in this area between ESA95 and its successor, ESA 2010.

So the UK had to size its drug trade and its sex trade. On 29 May 2014, Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew published the method.

Prostitution ran on annual volume of P times C times 52. P, the number of prostitutes, came from a 2004 charity estimate of "approx 7000 off-street prostitutes in London", reached "by calling numbers advertised in various media", plus a Metropolitan Police figure of "115 prostitutes on the street at any one time". "Adding these and scaling up by UK population ... gives c. 58,000 prostitutes." C, clients per week, followed "a Netherlands assumption". Price was £55 in 2004, "an estimate from Punternet". The Punternet footnote links to Wikipedia.

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS published every assumption and labelled its own weakness.

The prostitute count "is a weak assumption". That "all prostitutes and pimps are self-employed" "is a controversial assumption". The drug table is blunter. On constant purity-adjusted consumption: "This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." On cannabis sourcing: "This is an arbitrary assumption." On who buys: "The government final consumption expenditure assumption is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

"probably false" appears 3 times in the article. "arbitrary" appears 2 times.

## The bit continues

Peter Patterson, ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote on the Civil Service Quarterly blog that "ONS desk research ... yielded the result that spending on these illegal activities in the UK contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP". "Comparability between nations dictates that all countries include it in their GDP figures."

In Blue Book 2016 ONS reduced prostitution spending "by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes". "Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

2 published prostitution figures for 2010 result: £5.6bn in a 2015 FOI reply, £4,370m in today's series. 4,370 divided by 5,600 is 0.78; 40 working weeks out of 52 is 0.77. That arithmetic is this site's, not ONS's.

The series starts in 1985. It fell from £5,993m in 2019 to £4,130m in 2020, then reached £6,142m in 2022. It is still published; the last release was dated 31 October 2025.
