## The incident

On 29 May 2014 ONS published "Changes to National Accounts: Inclusion of Illegal Drugs and Prostitution in the UK National Accounts", by Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew. The rule was not new. ESA95 already counted consensual illegal transactions as production, and the article notes "no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA10." The timing came from the EU budget: GNI "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget", and a 2012 audit had set areas to "address by 2014."

The article warned of "significant limitations in the availability of data". The estimates were "partly based" on assumptions. "This applies especially to the estimates of prostitution activity."

The count started with Eaves, a charity, which in 2004 estimated approx 7,000 off-street prostitutes in London "by calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". The Metropolitan Police added 115 on the street. Scaling up by population "gives c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country." Clients per week: 20, 25 and 30, "Following a Netherlands assumption with some research support". Price: £55 in 2004, "an estimate from Punternet". The footnote for Punternet is a Wikipedia URL.

"As there is no time series, we vary the point estimates in time using the male population." ONS: "This is a weak assumption".

Drugs ran on the same terms. "This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." "We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption." The government spending assumption was "reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

## Who lied

Nobody. Every assumption was printed next to its weakness. The article uses "probably false" 3 times and "arbitrary" twice.

In October 2014 Peter Patterson, ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote on the Civil Service Quarterly blog that "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!) into the numbers of prostitutes and the price of drugs" put the contribution at "up to £10 billion a year". He called it "legally mandated through the European System of Accounts 2010".

An ONS freedom of information reply later gave illegal production for 2010 as £9,097m, 0.58% of GDP. Prostitution alone: £5.6bn.

## The bit continues

Blue Book 2016 added a refinement. ONS reduced the prostitution estimates "by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes. Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

Series MNC8 now gives prostitution spending for 2010 as £4,370m. The FOI reply gave £5.6bn. By this site's arithmetic, the newer figure is 78% of the older, and 40 working weeks are 77% of 52. ONS has not said the holiday is the whole difference.

The series begins in 1985, 29 years before the first estimate. It reads £5,993m for 2019, £4,130m for 2020 and £6,431m for 2024. It was last released on 31 October 2025.
