## The incident

UK GDP counts illegal drugs and prostitution. The rule is in ESA95, section 1.13, which includes "illegal transactions to which all units involved consent". ONS says there are no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA 2010.

ONS says the estimates are "partly based" on assumptions. This applies "especially to the estimates of prostitution activity."

The prostitute count starts with a 2004 survey. Eaves, a charity, "estimated that there were approx 7000 off-street prostitutes in London", by "calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". The Metropolitan Police gave "115 prostitutes on the street at any one time in London". Adding those and scaling up by UK population gives "c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country."

The price is £55 in 2004, "an estimate from Punternet". Provenance problem: ONS's footnote for that price links to Wikipedia's article on Punternet.

The purity-adjusted amount of drugs consumed by the average user is assumed to stay constant. "This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." Half of cannabis is assumed to be imported and half home-grown. "This is an arbitrary assumption."

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS printed its assumptions and flagged the weak ones in the authors' own words. Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew wrote the article. The number of prostitutes is assumed to follow the 16+ male population: "This is a weak assumption."

The government assumption is "reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

In a blog post for Civil Service Quarterly in October 2014, Peter Patterson, ONS's deputy chief economic adviser, wrote that spending on illegal activities "contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP". He added: "Comparability between nations dictates that all countries include it in their GDP figures."

## The bit continues

ONS's Blue Book 2016 revisions made an allowance that reduced the prostitution estimates "by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes". Statistics Netherlands estimated "the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year", and "the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

An ONS freedom of information reply gave £5.6bn for prostitution in 2010. The current series gives £4,370m for the same year. This site's arithmetic, not ONS's: £4,370m is 78% of £5.6bn, and 40 of 52 weeks is 77%. ONS does not say the holiday accounts for the whole difference.

The series is still published. Its latest release, on 31 October 2025, covers 1985 to 2024. ONS's method reads: "As there is no time series, we vary the point estimates in time using the male population." The series is modelled, not observed. It shows £5,993m in 2019, £4,130m in 2020 and £6,142m in 2022. No ONS source found explains the 2020 fall.
