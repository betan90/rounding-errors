## The incident

Since 1995, the European System of Accounts has required member states to include "illegal transactions to which all units involved consent" within GDP's production boundary; ESA 2010 changed nothing on this point, ONS's own footnote says. GNI "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget," so a 2012 EU-wide audit of national-accounts methods set a 2014 deadline to fix the gaps, including illegal drugs and prostitution.

ONS published its method in May 2014, by Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew. For prostitution: a 2004 call-based count by the charity Eaves found approximately 7,000 off-street prostitutes in London; the Metropolitan Police counted "115 prostitutes on the street at any one time." Scaled to the national population, the total reached "c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country." Because "there is no time series," the figure is since varied using the male population, "a weak assumption." Clients per week come from "a Netherlands assumption," calculated 3 ways, at 20, 25 and 30; which version was published is not stated. The price, "£55 in 2004," moves with the CPI for personal services; its source is Punternet, footnoted to a Wikipedia article.

Table 3 lists its own drug assumptions: purity-adjusted consumption held constant over time ("probably false, but we have no data to challenge it"), half of UK cannabis assumed home-grown ("an arbitrary assumption"), and government drug spending assumed at 0 because "government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs." "Probably false" appears 3 times in the article; "arbitrary," 2.

## Who lied

Nobody. Every quoted weakness above is ONS's own description of its own method. It published the method, the price source, and the Wikipedia footnote together.

Peter Patterson, ONS's Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote up the result for Civil Service Quarterly that October: "these illegal activities in the UK contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP." His blog called the practice "legally mandated through the European System of Accounts 2010." ONS's own methodology article puts the mandate in ESA95 instead, and says ESA 2010 changed nothing.

## The bit continues

Blue Book 2016 added a further adjustment: prostitution's household-spending estimate was reduced "to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes," using a figure from Statistics Netherlands, which "estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year." ONS applied the same factor to the UK.

2 ONS figures for the same year now disagree: an FOI reply in 2015 put prostitution's 2010 contribution at £5.6bn; the published series, MNC8, gives 2010 as £4,370m. The ratio, 0.78, sits close to the 40 working weeks left by a 12-week holiday out of 52, 0.77. That arithmetic is this site's, not ONS's; the published record does not link the holiday to the gap.

The series is still running, last updated 31 October 2025. It opens at £1,078m in 1985, reaches £5,993m in 2019, falls to £4,130m in 2020, and reaches £6,431m by 2024. ONS has not said why 2020 fell.
