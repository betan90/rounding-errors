## The incident

In May 2014 an ONS article by Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew set out how illegal drugs and prostitution would enter the UK national accounts. Gross national income, it noted, "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget". In 2012 an audit of methods across EU countries had identified areas for improvement "which Member States have to address by 2014". The rule itself is older: ESA95, section 1.13, puts illegal transactions "to which all units involved consent" inside the production boundary. A footnote adds: "There are no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA10."

For prostitution, the count starts with Eaves, a charity, which in 2004 estimated about 7,000 off-street prostitutes in London by "calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". The Metropolitan Police figure was "115 prostitutes on the street at any one time in London". Added and scaled up by UK population, these give "c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country." Estimates were calculated on 20, 25 and 30 clients a week, following "a Netherlands assumption with some research support". The price, £55 in 2004, is "an estimate from Punternet supported by a 2004 journal article". Footnote 12, for Punternet, is Punternet's Wikipedia page.

"As there is no time series," the article says, "we vary the point estimates in time using the male population". The assumption is that the number of prostitutes "has the same pattern through time as the 16+ male population. This is a weak assumption".

For drugs, the average user's purity-adjusted consumption is held constant: "This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." Half of cannabis sold in the UK is taken as imported, half home-grown: "This is an arbitrary assumption." The government final consumption expenditure assumption "is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

## Who lied

Nobody. The executive summary warned of "significant limitations in the availability of data" and estimates "partly based" on assumptions, "especially" for prostitution. Each assumption quoted above comes with ONS's own assessment attached. The article "does not include numerical impacts".

In October 2014 Peter Patterson, ONS's Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote on the Civil Service Quarterly blog that "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!)" found spending on these illegal activities "contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP."

## The bit continues

ONS still publishes the prostitution estimate, as series MNC8, last released on 31 October 2025. It runs from 1985; ONS published its first estimate in 2014.

In Blue Book 2016 ONS reduced the prostitution estimates by a factor "to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes". "Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

For 2010, an ONS freedom of information reply puts household spending on prostitution at £5.6bn. MNC8 puts it at £4,370m.
