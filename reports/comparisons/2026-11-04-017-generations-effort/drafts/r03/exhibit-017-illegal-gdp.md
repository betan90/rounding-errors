## The incident

Gross national income determines each member state's contribution to the EU budget. In 2012 the European Commission audited how member states compiled it and identified improvements to be made by 2014. One requirement: include illegal activities.

The rule was not new. ESA95, section 1.13, had said that illegal transactions to which all parties consent belong in the production boundary. An ONS footnote: "There are no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA10."

In May 2014 ONS published its method. The executive summary: "significant limitations in the availability of data ... requires a number of assumptions on which the estimates are, therefore, partly based. This applies especially to the estimates of prostitution activity."

The method: multiply prostitutes by clients per week by 52 weeks, then vary by the male population and the CPI. There is no time series data for prostitute numbers, so the 2004 baseline carries forward.

The 2004 baseline: the Metropolitan Police estimated 115 prostitutes "on the street at any one time in London"; a charity called Eaves "estimated that there were approx 7000 off-street prostitutes in London in that year; this was done by calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". ONS added these and scaled by UK population. Result: c. 58,000.

The price: "an estimate from Punternet supported by a 2004 journal article. This is £55 in 2004." Footnote 12 cites Punternet via http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punternet.

For drugs, ONS published Table 3: its assumptions and their quality. One: "We assume that the purity-adjusted amount of drugs consumed by the average user remains constant over time. This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." Another: "We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption." A third: "The government final consumption expenditure assumption is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS published its method, labelled the assumptions, and flagged which ones were weak, arbitrary, or probably false.

Patterson, then Deputy Chief Economic Adviser at ONS, wrote on the Civil Service Quarterly blog: "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!) into the numbers of prostitutes and the price of drugs." Illegal activities, he wrote, contributed "up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP".

## The bit continues

In Blue Book 2016 ONS refined the prostitution estimate: "allowance was made to reduce the estimates made for prostitution in household final consumption expenditure by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes. Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

The series continues. Its latest release, 31 October 2025, reports £6,431m for 2024.
