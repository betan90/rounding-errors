## The incident

On 29 May 2014 ONS set out how illegal drugs and prostitution would enter the UK national accounts. The article, by Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew, gave the reason: gross national income "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget", and a 2012 audit of methods across EU countries had identified areas "to address by 2014".

In 2004 the charity Eaves "estimated that there were approx 7000 off-street prostitutes in London in that year; this was done by calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". The Metropolitan Police supplied "115 prostitutes on the street at any one time in London". "Adding these and scaling up by UK population ... gives c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country."

Clients per week followed "a Netherlands assumption with some research support"; ONS calculated estimates "based on 20, 25 and 30." The price, £55 in 2004, was "an estimate from Punternet supported by a 2004 journal article". Footnote 12, for Punternet, is its Wikipedia page.

Assumption 1 tied the count to the male population aged 16 and over: "This is a weak assumption ... It is necessary because we have no time series data for the number of prostitutes."

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS published every assumption and graded several itself.

On drugs: "We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption." "We assume that the purity-adjusted amount of drugs consumed by the average user remains constant over time. This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." And: "The government final consumption expenditure assumption is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

The article uses "probably false" 3 times and "arbitrary" twice. It "does not include numerical impacts".

In October 2014 Peter Patterson, ONS's Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote on the Civil Service Quarterly blog: "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!) into the numbers of prostitutes and the price of drugs yielded the result that spending on these illegal activities in the UK contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP."

## The bit continues

Blue Book 2016 reduced the prostitution estimates "by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes. Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

ONS's prostitution series, MNC8, last released on 31 October 2025, gives 2010 as £4,370m. An ONS Freedom of Information reply gives 2010 as "£5.6bn (current prices)". This site's arithmetic, not ONS's: £4,370m is 78% of £5.6bn, and 40 weeks, 52 less 12 of holiday, are 77% of 52. None of the ONS sources here attributes the difference.

The series runs from 1985 to 2024. It fell 31%, from £5,993m in 2019 to £4,130m in 2020. ONS first estimated it in 2014.
