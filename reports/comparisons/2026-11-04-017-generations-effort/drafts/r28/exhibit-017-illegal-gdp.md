## The incident

In 2014 the Office for National Statistics added illegal drugs and prostitution to GDP. The reason is budgetary. GNI "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget", and a 2012 EU audit identified areas Member States "have to address by 2014". So ONS published the method.

The prostitution estimate starts from 1 count. The charity Eaves "estimated that there were approx 7000 off-street prostitutes in London" in 2004, "by calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". Scaled by UK population, that gave "c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country". The price was "£55 in 2004", varied forward by the CPI. The price source was Punternet, footnoted, in footnote 12, to a Wikipedia URL.

There was no series to extend. "As there is no time series, we vary the point estimates in time using the male population." ONS called that "a weak assumption".

## Who lied

Nobody. Every figure above is in the published article, with the authors flagging each soft spot themselves.

The drugs section is a table of caveats. "We assume that the purity-adjusted amount of drugs consumed by the average user remains constant over time. This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." "We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption." And the one that reads like satire and is not: "The government final consumption expenditure assumption is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs." The phrase "probably false" appears 3 times.

Patterson, then ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote on his gov.uk blog that "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!)" put illegal activity at "up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP". The italics are his.

## The bit continues

The method got a refinement, not a repair. In Blue Book 2016 ONS reduced the prostitution estimate "by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes". Statistics Netherlands "estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates".

The effect is visible in 2 published figures. A 2015 FOI reply gave 2010 prostitution as £5.6bn. Today's MNC8 series gives 2010 as £4,370m. ONS does not state what accounts for the gap.

The series is still published. The latest MNC8 release is dated 31 October 2025, and runs back to 1985, 29 years before the first estimate existed. 2019 reads £5,993m, 2020 £4,130m, 2022 £6,142m. The chart is a line drawn through one assumed point.
