## The incident

The European System of National Accounts puts illegal transactions to which all parties consent inside the production boundary. The rule is not new. ONS's footnote: "There are no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA10." What changed was enforcement. Gross national income "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget", and a 2012 EU audit of members' methods set a 2014 deadline.

In 2014 ONS added illegal drugs and prostitution to the UK national accounts. The method, published on 29 May by Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew, flagged "significant limitations in the availability of data" that "requires a number of assumptions ... This applies especially to the estimates of prostitution activity".

The prostitution count began with a 2004 survey by the charity Eaves, which found about 7,000 off-street prostitutes in London, counted "by calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". The Metropolitan Police gave "115 prostitutes on the street at any one time". "Adding these and scaling up by UK population ... gives c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country." The price per visit was "£55 in 2004", from Punternet, which the article footnoted to Wikipedia. Clients per week came from "a Netherlands assumption", at 20, 25 and 30.

The drugs method labelled its own inputs. On cannabis: "This is an arbitrary assumption." On government spending: "government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS published every assumption and marked the weak ones. "probably false" appears 3 times in the article; "arbitrary" twice. The number of prostitutes "has the same pattern through time as the 16+ male population. This is a weak assumption." On labour: "all prostitutes and pimps are self-employed. This is a controversial assumption."

Peter Patterson, then ONS's deputy chief economic adviser, summarised the result on a government blog: "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!) into the numbers of prostitutes and the price of drugs yielded the result that spending on these illegal activities in the UK contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP."

## The bit continues

In Blue Book 2016 ONS refined the prostitution estimate. "Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

2 published figures bracket the change. A 2015 FOI reply put 2010 prostitution at £5.6bn. The current series puts the same year at £4,370m. ONS does not say the holiday is the whole difference, and neither does this.

The series is still released, the latest vintage dated 31 October 2025.
