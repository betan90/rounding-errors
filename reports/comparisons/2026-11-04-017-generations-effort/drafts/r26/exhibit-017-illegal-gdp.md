## The incident

ONS added illegal drugs and prostitution to UK GDP in 2014. The reason, per ONS's own article: gross national income "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget". The rule was old: a footnote in the article confirms "there are no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA10."

For prostitution, ONS built a volume estimate from a 2004 charity count of London brothels and a police street count, "scaling up by UK population ... gives c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country." Clients per week came from "a Netherlands assumption with some research support ... we calculate estimates based on 20, 25 and 30." The price, "an estimate from Punternet supported by a 2004 journal article. This is £55 in 2004," is footnoted to Wikipedia.

For drugs, ONS graded its own assumptions in a published table. "We assume that half of cannabis sold in the UK is imported and half home-grown. This is an arbitrary assumption." On government spending: "The government final consumption expenditure assumption is reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs." The phrase "probably false" appears 3 times in the article; "arbitrary" twice.

## Who lied

Nobody. ONS's summary states the weakness up front: "significant limitations in the availability of data ... requires a number of assumptions on which the estimates are, therefore, partly based. This applies especially to the estimates of prostitution activity." Authors Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew published the method, not a defense.

In October 2014, ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser Peter Patterson summarised the result on a gov.uk blog: "ONS desk research ... into the numbers of prostitutes and the price of drugs yielded the result that spending on these illegal activities in the UK contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP." Counting R&D too, he wrote, the effect was to "raise the level of GDP by around £50 billion a year".

An ONS FOI response later gave prostitution's 2010 value as £5.6bn. The series ONS now publishes gives 2010 as £4,370m. Both figures are ONS's, for the same year.

## The bit continues

In 2016, ONS refined the model again: "allowance was made to reduce the estimates made for prostitution ... by a factor to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes. Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

4,370 divided by 5,600 is 0.78, close to the 40 working weeks a 12-week holiday leaves from 52: 0.77. ONS has not said the holiday accounts for the whole gap; that division is this site's, not ONS's.

The series, MNC8, is still published, last released 31 October 2025: 1,078 in 1985, 6,431 in 2024, a fall to 4,130 in 2020, unexplained. ONS's method says plainly: "there is no time series." The chart is one 2004 estimate, walked forward by population and inflation.
