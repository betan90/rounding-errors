## The incident

In May 2014 the Office for National Statistics (ONS) published a change that put illegal drugs and prostitution into the UK national accounts. The stated reason was that GNI "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget".

The rule was already there. ESA95, section 1.13, includes "illegal transactions to which all units involved consent". ONS says ESA 2010 kept it: "There are no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA10."

ONS has no time series data for the number of prostitutes. Its method says: "As there is no time series, we vary the point estimates in time using the male population." Eaves, a charity, estimated about 7,000 off-street prostitutes in London in 2004, "by calling numbers advertised in various media". The Metropolitan Police counted 115 on the street at any given moment. Adding both counts and scaling up by UK population "gives c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country."

ONS calculated estimates on 20, 25 and 30 clients per week. The basis was "a Netherlands assumption with some research support". The article does not say which it used. The price was "an estimate from Punternet supported by a 2004 journal article": £55 in 2004. ONS's footnote for Punternet points to Wikipedia.

Half of cannabis was assumed imported and half home-grown. "This is an arbitrary assumption." ONS calls its government assumption "reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

## Who lied

Nobody hid anything. The assumptions are in the published method, and ONS labels some of them itself. On drug consumption it says: "This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it."

The article says it "does not include numerical impacts". In October 2014, Peter Patterson, ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote on the Civil Service Quarterly blog that ONS desk research found spending on drugs and prostitution "contributes up to £10 billion a year to the level of GDP".

## The bit continues

The series, which runs from 1985 to 2024, is still published. The ONS page gives 31 October 2025 as its release date. The chart's line is modelled, not observed. ONS varies the 2004 count by the male population, and the price by the CPI for personal services.

The series falls from 5,993 in 2019 to 4,130 in 2020. No ONS source read for this entry explains the fall.

The 2016 Blue Book added "a further refinement": a factor "to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes". Statistics Netherlands estimated the holiday at 12 weeks per year, and "the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

A Freedom of Information reply gave the 2010 value as £5.6bn, at current prices. Today's series gives £4,370m for 2010. ONS does not say the holiday is the whole difference.
