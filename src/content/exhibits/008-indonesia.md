## The incident

Indonesian deforestation really did fall after 2015, and nobody serious disputes it. What varies is by how much, and the honest answer turns out to be a shrug of about fifty percentage points wide.

The government's figure is 90%. Researchers at the Norwegian University of Life Sciences went looking for that number in the independent satellite datasets and found declines, but smaller ones: Global Forest Watch showed 69%. Across every dataset they examined, the measured reduction ranged from 40% to 90% depending entirely on which data you used and which years you compared. Their own robust estimate: deforestation has fallen by at least 50% since around 2016.

The single biggest lever is the starting year. The official comparison runs from 2015, which was an extreme El Niño, hotter and drier than normal, the year Indonesia's peat and forest fires were among the worst on record. Measured against your worst year, any subsequent year looks like a triumph.

## Who lied

Nobody, and the researchers who raised it went out of their way to say so. 2015 is "not necessarily incorrect" as a baseline, one of the NMBU co-authors noted, because it was also the first full year of the Jokowi administration, which is a perfectly ordinary reason for a government to start counting there.

The deeper problem isn't the baseline, it's the word. Indonesia's forestry ministry counts deforestation as permanent conversion of forest to non-forest, minimum 6.25 hectares, identified by manual visual inspection of satellite images, on a July-to-June year. Global Forest Watch measures tree cover loss on a calendar year against a fixed baseline, includes plantations, and excludes young secondary forest. Indonesia's official definition of "forest" includes commercial acacia plantations grown expressly to be cut down for pulp.

An independent Indonesian monitor, Auriga Nusantara, put it about as cleanly as it can be put: all the data are valid as long as each adheres to its own methodology. Methodology is what sets these datasets apart.

## The bit continues

In 2025, Indonesian deforestation rose 66%, reversing years of decline, with nickel mining for electric vehicle batteries among the drivers. The sources disagree about the size of the increase, for exactly the same reasons they disagreed about the size of the decrease.
