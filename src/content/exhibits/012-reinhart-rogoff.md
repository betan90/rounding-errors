## The incident

In May 2010 Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff published "Growth in a Time of Debt". Covering 20 advanced economies from 1946 to 2009, it found that "median growth rates for countries with public debt over roughly 90 percent of GDP are about one percent lower than otherwise; (mean) growth rates are several percent lower." The published average above 90% was -0.1%.

The paper travelled. By April 2013 the authors' website listed 76 high-profile media features, and a Google Scholar search returned 538 results, excluding the authors' own. Thomas Herndon, Michael Ash and Robert Pollin (HAP) reported that it was the only evidence cited on debt and growth in the "Paul Ryan Budget", whose "Path to Prosperity" called it "conclusive empirical evidence" that debt over 90% "has a significant negative effect on economic growth."

HAP could not reproduce the result from the public data. The authors sent them the working spreadsheet. With it, HAP first closely approximated the published result, then corrected it. They got 2.2%.

## Who lied

Nobody, on the record. HAP's paper describes 3 problems: coding errors, selective exclusion of available data, and unconventional weighting of summary statistics.

The coding error: a formula averaged lines 30 to 44 instead of 30 to 49, which dropped Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada and Denmark entirely. "The omitted countries are selected alphabetically."

The exclusion: available early years were left out for Australia, Canada and New Zealand. New Zealand's excluded high-debt years grew 7.7, 11.9, -9.9 and 10.8 percent. What remained was 1 year, 1951, at -7.6%.

The weighting: each country was averaged first, then every country counted equally. Britain contributed 19 years averaging 2.4%. New Zealand contributed its 1 year. With 7 countries in the bucket, that year carried 14.3% of the weight. An apparent transcription error then moved it to -7.9%.

The paper reported 96 country-years above 90%. HAP found 110. The calculation used 71.

HAP's Table 3 runs every combination. Starting from the correct data, any 1 error alone moves the result 0.3 of a point, to 1.9%. Any 2 together move it to between 0.3% and 1.7%. All 3 take it to 0.0%. The transcription error supplies the last 0.1.

## The fix

On 5 May 2013 the authors published an erratum thanking HAP "for pointing out an important correction". It concedes "the coding error pointed out to us by HAP", adds missing years for Spain, and includes New Zealand's 1948 and 1949, which it describes as "a coding correction". It also adds a second New Zealand GDP series beside the Maddison series the 2010 paper used. Its final postwar average above 90% is 2.0% on New Zealand's historical statistics and 1.0% on the Maddison series.

The erratum contests the framing. It notes that HAP is silent on the longer sample back to the 1800s and on emerging markets, and that HAP "fails to mention that our median estimates for the postwar sample are very close in magnitude to the averages that they present."
