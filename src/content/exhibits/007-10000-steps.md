## The incident

After the 1964 Tokyo Olympics, Japan had a fitness wave and a clockmaker had an opening. In 1965, Yamasa Tokei Keiki released a pedometer called the *Manpo-kei*: *man* for 10,000, *po* for steps, *kei* for meter. Ten-thousand-step meter. The figure was picked because it was round, because it fit the device's dial, and because the kanji for 10,000, 万, happens to look like a person mid-stride. It sold extremely well.

Then the number did the thing numbers occasionally do: it escaped the product. Sixty years later, a target chosen to move pedometers is treated by a large share of the planet as the line between a healthy day and a lazy one, printed into watches its inventors could not have imagined.

## Who lied

Nobody. A brand name is not a claim made under oath, and Yamasa never pretended it was running a clinical trial. The failure here isn't a lie, it's that for six decades essentially no one asked for the study, because a confident round number rarely gets asked.

I-Min Lee, a Harvard epidemiologist who studies steps and mortality, put the provenance plainly: when the device launched, "There were no actual studies that had looked at '10,000 steps.'" The figure was a slogan, chosen because it sounded good and was easy to remember. From there it laundered upward, promoted in the United States by a former Surgeon General in the 1990s, and outward onto every fitness tracker sold, none of which cite a source, because there was never one to cite.

## The fix

When people who weren't selling pedometers finally checked, the answer came in below 10,000 and stayed there. Lee's 2019 study in *JAMA Internal Medicine* tracked roughly 17,000 older women: mortality risk fell steadily as daily steps rose, then flattened at about 7,500. Women who took 7,500 steps had the same mortality risk as women who took 10,000. A 2022 *Lancet Public Health* meta-analysis of 15 cohorts found the same plateau, shifting a little with age, roughly 6,000 to 8,000 steps for older adults.

The benefit of walking is real and well-measured. The specific number 10,000 is simply not where that benefit lives, and was never chosen to be.

## The bit continues

The number is more entrenched now than ever, because the pedometer became the smartwatch. Ten thousand ships as the default goal on the major fitness trackers, a ring to close, a badge to earn, a red streak to feel guilty about. A 1965 Japanese sales figure is now enforced every day by several hundred million wrists. The marketing outlived the marketers and quietly annexed the medicine.
