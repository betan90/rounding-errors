## The incident

In the BMJ's 1981 Christmas issue, T.J. Hamblin published "Fake!", on "Frauds, hoaxes, fakes, and widely popularised mistakes". 1 paragraph covered spinach: "German chemists reinvestigating the iron content of spinach had shown in the 1930s that the original workers had put the decimal point in the wrong place and made a tenfold overestimate of its value." It closed: "For a source of iron Popeye would have been better off chewing the cans."

The paragraph carries no citation. The article ends with 13 publications Hamblin "should particularly like to mention". Sutton, a Reader in Criminology at Nottingham Trent University, checked them in 2010: none cites "the source of the decimal error or 1930's Germans".

Raw spinach holds 2.71 mg of iron per 100 g, by the USDA's count. Dried spinach carries more per gram: Sutton cites a 1938 measurement of 44.8 mg. A 2009 textbook by Coultate, as Sutton quotes it, offers 2 versions: von Bunge found 35 mg per 100 g in 1890 from dried spinach, or von Wolf misplaced a decimal point about 20 years earlier. Rekdal reports that Sutton's follow-up attributed the 19th-century highs to "contamination during the analysis or the confusion between fresh and dried spinach". No decimal point required.

## Who lied

Nobody, on the record available. Sutton emailed Hamblin. Sutton reports that Hamblin "honestly cannot remember" his source, that the BMJ's then editor had asked him "not to provide references", and that he was "sure that he had not made it up." Sutton's 2010 paper concluded that, "in the absence of references to prove otherwise", Hamblin had invented the decimal error.

Then a reader pointed Sutton to Bender, whose 1972 inaugural lecture at Queen Elizabeth College, London, had said: "the fame of spinach may well have grown from a misplaced decimal point." Rekdal's reading: Hamblin "accidentally turned Bender's suggestion into a piece of fact". Hamblin's comment on Sutton's follow-up, as Rekdal quotes it: "I thought it was in Reader's Digest", and "very willing to admit that I was wrong."

Sutton also read the Popeye strips. By his reading, the strip of 3 July 1932, the first in which Popeye explains why he eats spinach, credits vitamin A.

## The bit continues

Kronick, in 1985, cited Hamblin and added a hedge: "It has been suggested that". Rekdal: such phrases "tend to change or disappear when knowledge passes from one text to another."

In 1995 the Journal of Internal Medicine published Larsson's "The dissemination of false data through inadequate citation". It repeated the story, citing Hamblin. Rekdal: Larsson "manages to do exactly what he is criticizing others for in quite a spectacular way."

Rekdal's own paper walks through 5 ways a writer might cite Larsson's spinach sentence. The fourth: "In short, I have plagiarized the Hamblin reference from Larsson." It is offered as a demonstration of the shortcut, not a confession.
