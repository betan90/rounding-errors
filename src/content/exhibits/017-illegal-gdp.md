## The incident

In 2014 the UK added illegal drugs and prostitution to its national accounts. Gross national income "is used in the calculation of a Member State's contribution to the EU budget", and a 2012 audit of EU methods had identified areas for improvement "which Member States have to address by 2014."

The rule was not new. ESA95, section 1.13, "states that illegal transactions to which all units involved consent are included within the production boundary". ONS added: "There are no changes in this area between ESA95 and ESA10."

ONS published the method on 29 May 2014, by Joshua Abramsky and Steve Drew. It warned of "significant limitations in the availability of data", requiring assumptions on which the estimates are "partly based". "This applies especially to the estimates of prostitution activity."

In 2004 a charity, Eaves, estimated about 7,000 off-street prostitutes in London, "by calling numbers advertised in various media and asking how many prostitutes were in each place". The Metropolitan Police estimated 115 on the street "at any one time". Scaled by population, that "gives c. 58,000 prostitutes in the whole country." Clients per week followed "a Netherlands assumption", run at 20, 25 and 30. The price, £55 in 2004, was "an estimate from Punternet supported by a 2004 journal article". Footnote 12, for Punternet, is a Wikipedia URL.

Constant drug consumption per user: "This assumption is probably false, but we have no data to challenge it." Half of cannabis imported, half home-grown: "This is an arbitrary assumption." No government spending on drugs: "reasonable because government units are very unlikely to spend money on illegal drugs."

## Who lied

Nobody. Every assumption was published, many with a grade attached. "Probably false" appears 4 times in the article. Tracking prostitution through time by the 16+ male population is "a weak assumption", needed "because we have no time series data for the number of prostitutes". Treating every prostitute and pimp as self-employed is "a controversial assumption".

In October 2014 Peter Patterson, ONS Deputy Chief Economic Adviser, wrote on the Civil Service Quarterly blog that "ONS desk research (please note the italics for emphasis!)" put the contribution at "up to £10 billion a year". ESA 2010 was "legally mandated", and "Comparability between nations dictates that all countries include it in their GDP figures."

## The bit continues

Blue Book 2016 reduced the prostitution estimates "to reflect the holiday taken by prostitutes". "Statistics Netherlands have estimated the total holidays to be 12 weeks per year and the same factor has been applied to the UK estimates."

An ONS freedom of information reply of November 2015 gives prostitution in 2010 as "£5.6bn (current prices)". Series MNC8 now gives 2010 as £4,370m. ONS does not attribute the difference. This site's arithmetic: £4,370m is 78% of £5.6bn, and 40 weeks is 77% of 52.

MNC8 was last released on 31 October 2025. It shows £5,993m for 2019, £4,130m for 2020 and £6,431m for 2024.
