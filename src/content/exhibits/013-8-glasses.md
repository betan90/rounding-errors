## The incident

"The advice to drink at least eight glasses of water a day can be found throughout the popular press," Vreeman and Carroll wrote in the BMJ in 2007. The standard version is 8 glasses of 8 ounces: 64 ounces, about 1.9 litres. It usually arrives with an amendment, recorded by Heinz Valtin in 2002: beverages containing caffeine and alcohol do not count.

## Who lied

Nobody, on the papers' own hedged account. Vreeman and Carroll offer "one origin" that "may" explain the rule: a 1945 recommendation, which the American Physiological Society's magazine attributes to the US Food and Nutrition Board. As quoted in the BMJ: "A suitable allowance of water for adults is 2.5 litres daily in most instances... Most of this quantity is contained in prepared foods."

Their reading: "If the last, crucial sentence is ignored, the statement could be interpreted as instruction to drink eight glasses of water a day." 2.5 litres is about 10.6 8-ounce glasses, most of it already in food.

Their second candidate is the nutritionist Frederick Stare, who recommended, "without references," "around 6 to 8 glasses per 24 hours," which could be "in the form of coffee, tea, milk, soft drinks, beer, etc." Stare counted coffee. The modern version excludes it.

This site did not see the 1945 document or Stare's book. Both are quoted as the BMJ reproduces them, and the origin is a hypothesis in both papers, not a documented chain.

## The fix

Valtin searched electronic databases and made "a cursory examination of the older literature," and consulted nutritionists who specialize in thirst. His finding, for healthy, sedentary adults in a temperate climate: "No scientific studies were found in support of 8 x 8." Published studies also contradicted the amendment: caffeinated drinks, and to a lesser extent beer in moderation, may count. Since it is "difficult or impossible to prove a negative," he invited readers to send any pertinent publications.

In 2004 the Institute of Medicine set adequate intakes for adults aged 19 to 30 at 2.7 litres of total water for women and 3.7 for men, the median intakes in the NHANES III survey, with about 20% coming from food. The report states "there is no single daily total water requirement for a given person." The panel's chair, Lawrence Appel: "We don't offer any rule of thumb based on how many glasses of water people should drink each day." The release opened with the report's finding: "The vast majority of healthy people adequately meet their daily hydration needs by letting thirst be their guide."

The 2007 BMJ paper listed the rule as 1 of 7 medical myths, citing Valtin's review as an exhaustive catalogue of "the complete lack of evidence."

## The bit continues

The NHS page on water, last reviewed in May 2023, says the UK government recommends that people "aim to drink 6 to 8 cups or glasses of fluid a day." The next sentence: "This is just a guide." Water, lower-fat milk, and lower-sugar drinks, including tea and coffee, all count.
